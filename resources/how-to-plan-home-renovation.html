How Do You Plan a Home Renovation?

To plan a home renovation, first define the problems you want to solve and what success looks like. Then establish a complete project budget, choose the right design and construction team for your needs, develop the scope and plans, compare proposals carefully, prepare your home and decisions for construction, and document the project through final closeout. The process is rarely perfectly linear, but making decisions in this order reduces avoidable uncertainty and rework.

It is easy to begin with the visible parts of a renovation: cabinets, flooring, fixtures, appliances, or paint. Those decisions matter, but they are not the best starting point. Early choices about goals, scope, budget, team structure, and timing shape nearly every decision that follows.

Every home, household, and renovation is different. A process that works well for one homeowner may not fit another homeowner's priorities, project complexity, desired level of involvement, or tolerance for disruption. The purpose of this guide is not to prescribe one “right” way to renovate. It is to help you understand the decisions ahead, ask stronger questions, and choose an approach that fits your project.

This is general homeowner education, not legal, architectural, engineering, inspection, financial, or safety advice. Project requirements should be confirmed with the appropriate qualified professionals and government agencies.

Already Started Your Renovation?

You can use this guide at any stage. If you already have plans, bids, or active construction, identify the Roadmap phase that best matches your next decision. Then check whether an earlier unanswered question—such as an unclear priority, incomplete scope, or missing budget item—is creating uncertainty now.

A successful renovation is a sequence of connected decisions. The Foundation Renovation Roadmap™ organizes those decisions into six phases: Discover, Plan, Design, Prepare, Build, and Complete.

The phases provide structure, not a rigid formula. They may overlap, and new information may send you back to revisit an earlier assumption. For example, design work can reveal a cost or permitting issue that changes the scope. The important thing is to recognize those connections before a decision becomes difficult or expensive to change.

01

Discover: Define Why You're Renovating

Before deciding what to build, define why you are considering a renovation. Focus first on the problems in the current home and the outcomes your household needs.

Identify the problems to solve

Describe what is not working without jumping immediately to a design solution. “The kitchen becomes congested when two people cook” is more useful than “we need a large island.” The first statement leaves room to evaluate several possible solutions.

Define what success looks like

Think about how the finished home should support daily life. Consider who lives there now, how the household may change, how long you expect to remain, and which improvements would have the greatest practical effect.

Separate needs from preferences

Organize ideas into Must Have, Nice to Have, and Future Phase. This creates a decision framework if design, budget, or schedule constraints require tradeoffs later.

Questions to discuss

  • What is not working in the home today?
  • Which outcomes are essential?
  • Why is now the right time to renovate?
  • How long do you expect to remain in the home?
  • What would make the completed project feel successful?
  • Which disruptions or temporary arrangements would be hardest for your household?
02

Plan: Establish Priorities and a Realistic Budget

A useful renovation budget covers the whole project, not only the contractor's construction price. It should help you evaluate options and make tradeoffs rather than simply record a desired number.

Build a whole-project budget

Depending on the project, costs outside the construction contract may include design and engineering, surveys, permit and plan-review fees, testing, owner-purchased fixtures or appliances, temporary housing, moving and storage, financing, landscaping, window coverings, furnishings, and closeout work. Identify which party is carrying each cost so the same item is not omitted—or counted twice.

Plan for uncertainty without relying on one universal percentage

Existing conditions, incomplete design decisions, price changes, and homeowner-requested changes can all affect cost. The appropriate reserve depends on the scope, how much is known about the home, the completeness of the documents, and the risks identified by the project team. Ask what the budget assumes, what remains unknown, and how an unexpected condition would be evaluated.

Test priorities against the budget

Do not wait until bids arrive to decide what can change. Identify lower-priority scope, finish, or phasing options in advance. A thoughtful alternative should still support the goals defined during Discover.

Consider the cost of disruption

Living in the home may reduce temporary-housing expenses, but it can affect safety, privacy, access, household routines, and construction efficiency. Moving out can simplify some work while adding rent, moving, storage, travel, and pet-care costs. Discuss the tradeoffs with the project team before assuming either option will be practical.

03

Design: Develop the Right Solution and Team

Architects, interior designers, engineers, contractors, specialty consultants, and design-build firms serve different roles. The right combination depends on the scope, technical complexity, approvals, design expectations, budget, and how you prefer to participate in decisions.

Understand the available team structures

An architect-led process may suit a project that needs extensive architectural design or coordination. An interior designer may lead finish-intensive or interior-focused work. A contractor-led approach may fit some clearly defined projects. A design-build firm can combine design and construction responsibility. Other projects use a collaborative team assembled from independent professionals.

None of these structures is universally best. Ask who is responsible for design, technical documentation, engineering, estimating, permits, selections, coordination, construction, and decisions—and understand where your responsibilities begin and end.

Develop enough information to make informed decisions

Plans should communicate the intended work clearly enough for the next decision. The appropriate level of documentation varies, but requesting contractor proposals from inconsistent or incomplete information makes comparisons harder and can leave important assumptions unresolved.

Check design against goals and budget

Review the developing design at planned milestones. Confirm that it still solves the original problems, supports household priorities, and aligns with current budget assumptions. A beautiful design that cannot be permitted, priced, or built within the project's constraints is not yet a complete solution.

If you want an independent review of how plans align with your stated priorities, Foundation describes its homeowner-focused support in the Plan Review™.

04

Prepare: Get Ready for Construction

Prepare is the bridge between design intent and construction. This is the time to clarify approvals, compare proposals, review contract terms, make critical selections, establish communication procedures, and prepare the household.

Confirm permits and local requirements

Permit requirements depend on the jurisdiction and scope. The City of San Diego Development Services Department states that permits are commonly required for remodeling and for repairs to electrical, mechanical, and plumbing systems. Its current residential addition and remodel guidance also identifies project-specific considerations such as structural documentation, zoning, historic review, fire sprinklers, inspections, and other regulations.

A San Diego mailing address does not necessarily mean the City of San Diego is the permitting authority. Confirm whether the property is within the city, another incorporated city, or unincorporated San Diego County. Coastal, historic, environmental, wildfire, and other site conditions may also affect review. Verify the current requirements with the governing agency and qualified project professionals before relying on schedule or fee assumptions.

Compare contractors and proposals carefully

Confirm that each contractor is evaluating substantially the same plans, specifications, selections, and scope. Compare included work, exclusions, allowances, alternates, schedule assumptions, supervision, payment terms, and change procedures—not just the total price.

California homeowners can verify a contractor's license and review current hiring guidance through the Contractors State License Board. CSLB recommends comparing written bids based on consistent plans and specifications.

Understand the contract before signing

Read the complete agreement and ask questions about anything unclear. Confirm the scope, documents incorporated into the agreement, start and completion provisions, payment schedule, allowances, exclusions, change-order process, cleanup, dispute terms, and responsibilities for permits and owner-purchased items.

California home-improvement contract, payment, cancellation, lien, and change-order requirements are legal matters. Consult current CSLB homeowner contract guidance and obtain legal advice when appropriate rather than relying on a general renovation article.

Verify insurance rather than assuming coverage

Ask for current insurance documentation and understand what it represents. Workers' compensation and commercial general liability are different coverages, and requirements can depend on the contractor's circumstances. CSLB advises homeowners to check workers' compensation information and consult their own insurance carrier about the adequacy of coverage. Verify current requirements and coverage directly with CSLB, the contractor's insurer, and your own insurance professional.

Resolve selections and long-lead decisions

Identify which products must be selected or ordered before work begins, who will purchase them, where they will be stored, and what happens if they arrive late or damaged. Avoid assuming every finish decision can wait until construction.

Prepare the household

Discuss access, work hours, parking, dust control, utilities, security, pets, children, belongings, temporary facilities, and communication expectations. If the home will remain occupied, ask the contractor and appropriate safety professionals which areas can be used safely during each phase.

Older-home health and safety

Renovation can disturb hazardous materials. For many pre-1978 homes, federal lead-safe requirements apply to paid work that disturbs painted surfaces; review current EPA guidance for homeowners. San Diego-area asbestos requirements should be checked with the San Diego County Air Pollution Control District. Do not test, disturb, remove, or dispose of suspected hazardous material based only on general web guidance—consult appropriately qualified professionals and the governing agencies.

05

Build: Stay Informed During Construction

You do not need to direct trades or take over the contractor's responsibilities to remain an informed homeowner. Focus on timely decisions, clear records, understood changes, and communication through the agreed project channels.

Establish a useful communication rhythm

Agree on who communicates with whom, how often project meetings occur, where decisions are recorded, and how urgent questions are handled. Ask for explanations in homeowner language when technical information is unclear.

Document decisions and approvals

Keep organized records of major decisions, selections, questions, meeting notes, approved changes, payment documentation, and relevant photographs. Confirm verbal decisions in the communication system identified by the contract or project team.

Evaluate changes before authorizing them

A change may affect more than price. Ask why it is needed, what work it includes, what alternatives exist, how it changes the schedule, and whether it affects other parts of the design. Contract requirements govern how changes must be documented and approved; seek legal advice for contract disputes.

Review progress within the proper roles

Understand what information supports a payment request and raise questions promptly. Do not represent a homeowner observation as a professional inspection, code determination, or certification. Architects, engineers, inspectors, contractors, and other professionals retain their respective responsibilities.

Revisit household logistics

Conditions change during construction. Continue discussing access, utilities, dust barriers, deliveries, pets, children, and occupied areas. Follow the contractor's site rules and qualified safety guidance; active construction areas can contain hazards that are not obvious to a visitor.

06

Complete: Close Out the Project Carefully

The end of active construction is not automatically the end of the project. Allow time to document incomplete items, collect records, understand new systems, and confirm the agreed closeout process.

Conduct the final walkthrough

Prepare a clear list of visible incomplete, damaged, or unresolved items. Use specific locations and descriptions, add photographs when helpful, and track responsibility and status. A homeowner punch list is not a substitute for required government inspections or professional evaluations.

Collect closeout information

Depending on the project, closeout materials may include approved plans, permit and inspection records, warranties, manuals, product information, finish schedules, care instructions, spare materials, keys, access credentials, and contact information.

Understand remaining obligations

Review the contract and professional agreements for final-payment requirements, unresolved work, warranty procedures, and other closeout responsibilities. Obtain legal or professional advice when rights, compliance, safety, or technical performance are in question.

Plan for maintenance

Learn how new materials and systems should be operated and maintained. Record maintenance intervals and warranty deadlines so the finished renovation continues to perform as intended.

Common Home Renovation Planning Questions

Where should I start when planning a renovation?

Start by defining the problems you want to solve, your essential outcomes, your household constraints, and your financial boundaries. Those decisions provide a better foundation than beginning with finishes or asking contractors for prices before the scope is clear.

Should I hire a contractor or designer first?

It depends on the project's complexity, required professional services, desired level of design support, budget, and preferred delivery method. An architect-led, designer-led, contractor-led, design-build, or collaboratively assembled team may each be appropriate in different circumstances. Understand the roles and tradeoffs before choosing.

When should I request contractor bids?

Proposals are usually easier to compare when contractors receive sufficiently complete and consistent plans, specifications, selections, and scope information. If important details remain unresolved, identify how each bidder treated those assumptions.

Do I need a permit for my renovation?

Permit requirements vary by jurisdiction and scope. Contact the applicable local building department and qualified project professionals. Do not assume that work is exempt because it is entirely inside the home or because a similar project elsewhere did not require the same review.

How much contingency should I include?

There is no single percentage that fits every renovation. Consider the completeness of the design, known existing conditions, project complexity, pricing certainty, and remaining homeowner decisions. Discuss an appropriate reserve with qualified financial and project professionals.

Can I live in my home during construction?

Sometimes, but the answer depends on scope, utilities, dust and safety controls, children and pets, access, schedule, and household tolerance. Compare the practical and financial tradeoffs with the project team before deciding.

Your Next Renovation Decision

You do not need every answer on the first day. You do need a clear understanding of the next decision and how it affects what follows. Identify your current Foundation Renovation Roadmap™ phase, write down the questions still unresolved, and make the next commitment only after you understand the relevant options and tradeoffs.
