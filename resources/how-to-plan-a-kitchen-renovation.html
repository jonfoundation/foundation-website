Where Should You Start a Kitchen Renovation?

Start by documenting how the kitchen needs to work: who cooks, how people move through the room, what must be stored, which appliances support daily routines, and what currently causes frustration.

Then coordinate the layout, cabinetry, appliance specifications, utilities, lighting, ventilation, selections, budget, approvals, and temporary-kitchen plan before major construction commitments are made.

A kitchen is a tightly coordinated workspace. Cabinets, appliances, plumbing, electrical service, lighting, ventilation, structure, finishes, and circulation affect one another. Beginning with countertops, cabinet colors, or inspiration photos can lock in choices before the functional and technical relationships are understood.

This is the kitchen-specific companion to How to Plan a Home Renovation. Use the The Foundation Renovation Roadmap™: Six Phases of a Home Renovation for the overall phases; use this guide for the kitchen decisions within those phases.

This article provides general homeowner education, not project-specific design, engineering, code, permitting, environmental, accessibility, or safety advice. Qualified professionals and the governing agency should evaluate the actual home and proposed work.

Function Before Finishes

Describe what the kitchen must help people do before deciding what it should look like. Once workflow, storage, appliances, and system needs are understood, finish decisions have a clearer context.

Define How the Kitchen Should Function

Observe normal days rather than imagining only holidays or idealized routines. Note who prepares food, whether several people cook together, where groceries enter, where dishes accumulate, how children or guests use the room, and whether a passage through the kitchen interrupts work.

Create a short list of outcomes:

  • Problems that must be solved, such as inadequate preparation space, poor storage, or conflicting traffic
  • Activities the kitchen should support, including cooking, cleanup, homework, entertaining, or accessible use
  • Items that require storage and which should be reachable from each activity
  • Features that work well today and should not be lost
  • Future household needs worth considering without assuming they will occur

Rank outcomes before designing solutions. “Reduce collisions between cooking and cleanup” is more useful than “add an island,” because several layouts might solve the problem.

Study the Existing Kitchen and Adjacent Rooms

Record dimensions, doors, windows, ceiling changes, appliance locations, plumbing fixtures, vents, electrical equipment, visible damage, and relationships to dining, living, laundry, garage, and exterior areas. Existing drawings can help but should be verified against current conditions.

Ask appropriate professionals to investigate conditions that could affect feasibility: structure, walls proposed for alteration, plumbing and drainage, electrical capacity, gas service, ventilation paths, moisture, and materials that may require environmental assessment.

If the kitchen is part of broader work, coordinate it with the whole-home plan so flooring, systems, circulation, and finishes are not decided room by room in conflict.

Do not use visible observations alone to reach technical or safety conclusions.

Test Layout and Workflow Before Styling the Kitchen

Evaluate relationships among food storage, preparation, cooking, serving, cleanup, waste, and circulation. There is no universally correct triangle, island, aisle, or open-plan arrangement. Household use, room geometry, appliance operation, accessibility goals, and technical constraints determine what works.

Test each option by walking through specific routines:

  • Can the refrigerator open while someone passes?
  • Can the dishwasher be loaded without blocking the primary route?
  • Where do hot items land?
  • Can drawers, doors, and appliances operate simultaneously?
  • Where do groceries, small appliances, recycling, and cleaning supplies go?
  • Can more than one person use the kitchen without repeated conflicts?

Changes to walls, windows, doors, or fixture locations can affect structure, exterior conditions, systems, approvals, cost, and schedule. Have the relevant professionals evaluate those consequences before treating a preferred layout as final.

Coordinate Cabinetry and Storage With Real Items

Cabinetry converts the layout into precise dimensions, clearances, storage, and interfaces with nearly every trade. Inventory what needs to be stored and group it by use: food, cookware, dishes, countertop appliances, utensils, waste, linens, and occasional items.

Review cabinet drawings for:

  • Door and drawer operation, fillers, panels, finished ends, and hardware
  • Interior accessories and the dimensions of items they must hold
  • Appliance openings, ventilation components, utility access, and manufacturer clearances
  • Countertop support, overhangs, seams, edge conditions, and backsplash relationships
  • Lighting, outlets, switches, plumbing, and trim that intersect the cabinets
  • Transitions at ceilings, walls, floors, windows, and adjacent rooms

Confirm who measures, who approves shop drawings, and how field conditions are reconciled. Cabinet approval should not be isolated from appliance and utility coordination.

Select and Coordinate Appliances Early Enough

Appliance decisions affect more than appearance. Model-specific dimensions, door swings, ventilation, electrical or gas requirements, water and drain connections, required clearances, panels, trim kits, and installation instructions can change cabinetry and systems.

Maintain an appliance schedule with the manufacturer, model, dimensions, utility requirements, delivery status, responsible purchaser, installer, and current installation documents. Do not assume a nominal appliance width defines the required opening.

For new appliances, clarify ordering, delivery, storage, inspection, damage claims, installation, connection, and disposal responsibilities. If existing appliances will remain, verify their actual condition and specifications.

Coordinate Plumbing, Electrical, Gas, and Other Systems

The layout creates demands on the home’s systems. Sink and dishwasher positions affect water and drainage. Cooking and refrigeration equipment affect electrical or gas planning. Lighting, countertop equipment, and charging needs affect electrical design. Connected devices may affect low-voltage or wireless planning.

Qualified professionals should assess existing capacity, condition, routing, required protection, and applicable codes. A kitchen renovation does not automatically require every system to be replaced, but a cosmetic assumption should not conceal a system question.

Code, energy, ventilation, and permitting requirements vary by jurisdiction and project scope. Confirm what applies with qualified professionals and the governing authority rather than relying on a rule from another city or state.

California energy requirements

In California, applicable energy requirements depend on project scope and permit timing. The California Energy Commission identifies the 2025 Building Energy Efficiency Standards as applicable to permit applications submitted on or after January 1, 2026. Confirm the requirements for the proposed alterations with project professionals and the governing authority.

Plan Lighting and Ventilation as Systems

Lighting

Plan for the actual tasks and surfaces: preparation, cooking, cleanup, storage, circulation, and adjacent dining or living. Coordinate fixture locations with cabinets, doors, shelving, ceiling features, appliances, and controls.

Consider daylight, glare, shadowing, color rendering, and control locations, while relying on qualified professionals for electrical design and code requirements.

Ventilation

Cooking can introduce moisture, heat, odors, and pollutants. Equipment selection, airflow, duct routing, termination, makeup air, clearances, noise, and code obligations require coordinated professional evaluation.

A range hood selected after cabinetry and ceiling work are finalized can create avoidable conflicts. Coordinate the equipment and route early enough for the design, trades, and budget to respond.

The U.S. EPA's current remodeling guidance discusses kitchen and bathroom exhaust to the outdoors. Follow manufacturer instructions as part of that professional coordination.

Match the Team and Budget to the Scope

A kitchen may involve a contractor, architect or residential designer, interior or kitchen designer, engineer, cabinet supplier, appliance specialist, and specialty trades in different combinations. It does not automatically require all of them.

Clarify who will own the layout, cabinet drawings, appliance schedule, utility coordination, finish selections, permit documents, pricing information, ordering, field questions, and change communication.

Build a complete project budget, not only a cabinet or construction number. Consider applicable professional services, investigation, approvals, construction, appliances, fixtures, owner purchases, delivery and storage, temporary household arrangements, and project-specific uncertainty.

Make Selections in a Coordinated Sequence

Not every product must be selected on day one, but decisions with dimensional, utility, lead-time, or installation consequences need priority.

A useful sequence often moves from layout and major appliances to cabinetry, plumbing fixtures, lighting, surfaces, hardware, and decorative details—with iteration as information develops.

For each selection, record:

  • Exact product and finish
  • Required quantity and accessories
  • Responsible purchaser and installer
  • Approval status and order deadline
  • Expected delivery and storage plan
  • Documents, trades, and costs affected by the decision

Treat substitutions as coordination decisions, not merely color changes.

Plan How the Household Will Function During Construction

A temporary kitchen is not necessary or practical for every project, and some households may choose to live elsewhere. Evaluate the likely loss of sink, cooking, refrigeration, food storage, laundry, access, and dining functions against the proposed sequence. How Long Does a Kitchen Remodel Take? explains the project phases and schedule factors to review.

If remaining in the home is being considered, discuss separation from the work, utility interruptions, dust and noise, access routes, pets and children, food storage, cleanup, and what equipment can be used safely in the proposed temporary location.

Include temporary food, storage, moving, cleaning, parking, pet, or housing costs in the complete project budget when applicable.

Do not improvise electrical, gas, plumbing, or ventilation arrangements. Discuss temporary arrangements with the contractor and relevant safety professionals.

Confirm the Kitchen Is Ready for the Next Commitment

Before approving final design work, ordering major products, or signing a construction agreement, confirm that:

  • Functional goals and priorities are documented
  • The preferred layout has been tested against real routines
  • Cabinetry, appliances, utilities, lighting, and ventilation are coordinated at the needed level
  • Owner-purchased items and responsibilities are clear
  • Approval and professional responsibilities have been confirmed
  • The construction scope connects to the complete project budget
  • Household logistics are understood
  • Open decisions and assumptions have owners and deadlines

The goal is not to eliminate every unknown. It is to make the remaining unknowns visible enough that the next commitment is informed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I keep the existing kitchen layout?

Keeping it may reduce some work, but it is not automatically the right decision. Compare how well it supports your goals against the design, system, approval, cost, and disruption implications of alternatives.

Should cabinets or appliances come first?

Coordinate them together. Appliance specifications affect cabinet openings and utilities; cabinet planning affects available appliance locations. Model-specific information should be available before fabrication is approved.

Does every kitchen renovation need a permit?

No universal answer applies. Requirements depend on the jurisdiction and proposed work. Confirm directly with the governing authority and qualified project professionals before construction.

Do I need to move out during a kitchen renovation?

Not always. The decision depends on the scope, anticipated duration, utility interruptions, safe separation, household needs, and available alternatives. Evaluate the actual construction plan rather than relying on a general rule.

When should I compare contractor bids?

Compare bids when bidders can price sufficiently consistent information. If the scope, drawings, selections, allowances, and responsibilities differ, the totals may not represent the same project.

Should I start by visiting a kitchen showroom?

A showroom can help you understand products and options, but product browsing should be guided by functional goals, likely scope, budget context, and decision sequence. Avoid treating an early cabinet or finish conversation as a complete project plan.

Foundation Tip

Before thinking about finishes, think about function. The most successful kitchen renovations begin with a clear understanding of how the space should support daily life. Design and product decisions become easier once those goals are established.

Closing Thought

A coordinated kitchen plan connects daily routines to layout, storage, appliances, systems, lighting, ventilation, selections, budget, approvals, and household logistics. That makes design and pricing conversations more useful before construction begins.
