How Long Does a Kitchen Remodel Take?

Understand how design, cabinets, appliances, permits, procurement, construction and closeout shape a realistic kitchen-remodel schedule.

A kitchen remodel is rarely just a sequence of construction tasks. The schedule is shaped by design decisions, cabinet engineering, appliance specifications, permits, long-lead purchases, inspections and the order in which specialized trades can complete their work.

That is why two kitchens with similar square footage can have very different timelines. A cosmetic update that keeps the existing layout is fundamentally different from a remodel that moves walls, changes plumbing, adds structural work or requires a new electrical service.

This guide explains what controls a kitchen-remodel schedule and how homeowners can evaluate a proposed timeline without treating an early estimate as a guarantee.

Start With the Full Project Timeline

The visible construction period is only one part of the project. A complete kitchen-remodel timeline generally includes:

  • Scope and feasibility
  • Design and selections
  • Pricing and contracting
  • Permitting and approvals, when required
  • Procurement of cabinets, appliances and other long-lead products
  • Site preparation and temporary-kitchen planning
  • Construction and inspections
  • Punch work, final approvals and closeout

Many schedule problems begin when homeowners focus only on demolition and installation. Work that happens before demolition often determines whether construction can proceed efficiently.

For the broader principles that apply to renovation scheduling, critical paths and contingency, see KB-2217, How Long Does a Home Renovation Take?

Why Kitchen Timelines Vary

Kitchen projects differ in scope, complexity and decision load. The most important schedule variables usually include:

  • Whether the existing layout will remain or change
  • Whether walls, beams or openings require structural engineering
  • The amount of electrical, plumbing, gas or ventilation work
  • Whether the project requires permits and inspections
  • The cabinet type, manufacturer and level of customization
  • Appliance availability and installation requirements
  • Countertop material, templating and fabrication
  • Specialty tile, flooring, millwork or lighting
  • Hidden conditions discovered after demolition
  • How quickly the homeowner makes and confirms decisions
  • Whether the home will remain occupied during construction

A responsible schedule should reflect the actual scope and known constraints. A short schedule is not automatically a better schedule if it ignores design, procurement, inspections or realistic trade sequencing.

The Preconstruction Phase

Define the Scope and Layout

The schedule becomes more reliable once the team understands what is changing. Keeping sinks, appliances, walls and major utilities in their existing locations may reduce design and construction complexity. Moving them may improve the kitchen, but it can introduce engineering, code, permitting and trade-coordination work.

Before pricing or ordering begins, the team should confirm the intended layout, major dimensions, structural implications and the level of finish.

Complete the Design and Key Selections

A kitchen cannot be scheduled well when critical decisions remain open. Cabinet plans, appliance specifications, plumbing fixtures, lighting, flooring, tile and finish selections affect both pricing and installation.

Late selections can force redesign, create incompatible conditions or delay orders. The goal is not to decide every decorative detail immediately. It is to identify which choices affect dimensions, utilities, permits, price or the critical path and resolve those first.

Establish Pricing and the Contract

A bid is most useful when it is based on a sufficiently defined scope. If major assumptions remain unresolved, the construction schedule may be built on allowances and placeholders that later change.

The contract should identify major schedule assumptions, owner responsibilities, long-lead items, payment milestones and how changes will be handled. Foundation’s Bid Review service can help homeowners compare proposals, identify important exclusions and prepare focused questions without attempting to replace the contractor’s responsibility for means, methods or schedule.

Obtain Permits and Approvals

Permit requirements vary by jurisdiction and scope. Layout changes, structural work and significant electrical, plumbing, mechanical or gas work often require review and inspection. Condominium or neighborhood approvals may add another process.

Do not assume that a permit can be obtained instantly or that construction can begin while key corrections remain unresolved. The proposed schedule should separate design, permit review and construction instead of compressing them into one vague start date.

Procurement Often Controls the Start Date

Cabinetry Is Frequently the Critical Path

Cabinets are dimensional infrastructure for the kitchen. Their design affects appliances, fillers, panels, countertops, lighting, plumbing locations and finished clearances.

A cabinet order should not be released until the layout, field dimensions, appliance specifications, door style, finish and accessories are adequately confirmed. Revisions after release can be expensive and may reset production timing.

Cabinet delivery is also not the same as cabinet installation. Before installation, the team may need to:

  • Receive and inventory the shipment
  • Document damaged, missing or incorrect pieces
  • Complete framing, rough utilities, inspections and drywall
  • Verify that floors and walls are ready
  • Coordinate replacement parts or field modifications

A realistic schedule accounts for these steps instead of showing cabinet installation immediately after the truck arrives.

Appliances Affect Design and Utilities

Appliance specifications should be confirmed early. Refrigerators, ranges, cooktops, ovens, microwaves, dishwashers and ventilation equipment may require specific openings, clearances, electrical circuits, gas connections, water lines or exhaust paths.

Changing an appliance after cabinets or utilities are planned can create redesign and rework. Availability also matters: an appliance that cannot be delivered when needed may delay final installation or require a temporary plan.

Countertops Have Their Own Sequence

Many countertops cannot be finally measured until base cabinets are installed, secured and verified. After field templating, the fabricator still needs time to prepare slabs, complete shop work and schedule installation.

Sink, faucet, cooktop and accessory information may be needed before fabrication. Backsplash, plumbing trim and some appliance work may depend on countertop completion. This makes countertop templating and fabrication a common critical-path interval that should appear clearly in the schedule.

Specialty Materials and Fixtures

Tile, flooring, decorative lighting, custom hoods, specialty hardware and plumbing fixtures may have longer or less predictable availability than standard products. The team should identify required-on-site dates and confirm storage, inspection and return procedures.

A Typical Kitchen Construction Sequence

The exact order varies, but a kitchen remodel commonly follows this sequence:

  1. Protect the home and establish access, dust-control and temporary-kitchen measures
  2. Disconnect utilities and remove existing cabinets, finishes and appliances
  3. Investigate exposed conditions and confirm any required scope changes
  4. Complete structural framing or opening modifications
  5. Install or modify rough plumbing, electrical, gas, ventilation and mechanical systems
  6. Complete required rough inspections
  7. Close walls and ceilings; complete drywall and initial painting
  8. Prepare floors and install flooring at the appropriate point for the selected assembly
  9. Install, level and secure cabinets and related millwork
  10. Template, fabricate and install countertops
  11. Install backsplash and complete finish painting
  12. Install appliances, plumbing fixtures, lighting, trim and hardware
  13. Complete startup, testing, final inspections, punch work and closeout

This is a planning framework, not a universal recipe. Flooring, painting, countertop and appliance sequences can change based on the materials, cabinet design, site conditions and contractor’s methods.

Permits and Inspections Can Affect the Critical Path

Required inspections are usually tied to specific stages. Work may need to remain visible until the inspector approves it, and a correction may need to be completed before walls are closed or the next trade proceeds.

Inspection availability, correction notices and revised documents can affect the schedule. The contractor should explain which inspections are anticipated, who schedules them and what work depends on each approval.

Living Without a Working Kitchen

A kitchen remodel can disrupt meals, storage, cleaning and daily routines even when the rest of the home remains usable. Before demolition, plan for:

  • A safe temporary food-preparation area
  • Refrigeration and small appliances on appropriate electrical circuits
  • Dishwashing and access to water
  • Protected storage for food, cookware and household items
  • Pet and child safety
  • Dust, noise, deliveries and worker access
  • Periods when water, power or gas may be temporarily interrupted
  • A backup plan for meals and particularly disruptive workdays

Do not create a temporary kitchen that overloads circuits, blocks exits or places cooking equipment in an unsafe location. Coordinate the plan with the contractor.

Occupied-Home Constraints

When the home remains occupied, protection and daily cleanup take time. Limited access, restricted working hours, shared entrances and the need to maintain utilities can also change productivity.

These measures are not schedule waste. They are part of safely managing construction in an occupied home and should be reflected in expectations.

Common Causes of Kitchen-Remodel Delays

  • Incomplete design or unresolved cabinet details
  • Late homeowner selections or approvals
  • Cabinet revisions, shipping damage or missing components
  • Appliance substitutions after design coordination
  • Permit corrections or inspection delays
  • Hidden water damage, unsafe wiring, deteriorated plumbing or uneven framing
  • Unexpected structural conditions
  • Electrical-service or panel limitations
  • Countertop material issues or fabrication changes
  • Custom millwork, tile or specialty finish availability
  • Trade-sequencing conflicts
  • Owner-requested changes after work begins
  • Insufficient time for testing, corrections and punch work

Some delays can be reduced through planning; others cannot be known until work is opened. A strong process distinguishes preventable decision delays from legitimate field discoveries.

How to Evaluate a Contractor’s Proposed Timeline

A useful schedule should be detailed enough to show the project logic. Ask whether it identifies:

  • The assumptions behind the proposed start date
  • Who is responsible for design, selections, permits and purchasing
  • Which products must be ordered before demolition
  • The cabinet release, delivery and installation sequence
  • Appliance specification and delivery dates
  • The countertop template, fabrication and installation interval
  • Required inspections and dependent work
  • Owner decision deadlines
  • Allowances for hidden conditions and corrections
  • The substantial-completion, punch and closeout process

Ask what activities control the completion date and what would happen if one of them moves. The goal is not to demand certainty that the project cannot provide. It is to understand whether the schedule reflects the actual work.

Schedule Contingency Matters

A schedule should include reasonable contingency for known risks rather than relying on a perfect sequence with no interruptions. The appropriate amount depends on project complexity, permitting, customization, site conditions and product availability.

Contingency does not mean the team should accept avoidable delay. It means the homeowner’s personal plans should not depend on the most optimistic possible completion date.

Common Planning Mistakes

  • Treating the demolition date as the beginning of planning
  • Ordering cabinets before appliances and field dimensions are confirmed
  • Assuming cabinet delivery means immediate installation
  • Ignoring the time between countertop templating and installation
  • Leaving major selections until construction
  • Scheduling travel, guests or events around an unbuffered completion date
  • Comparing contractor timelines without comparing scope and assumptions
  • Planning to occupy a temporary kitchen without checking safety and electrical capacity
  • Expecting hidden conditions to be resolved without schedule impact
  • Treating punch work and final approvals as optional

A Practical Planning Sequence

  1. Define the desired outcome and budget framework
  2. Confirm feasibility, layout and major code or structural issues
  3. Complete cabinet design and coordinate appliance specifications
  4. Make schedule-critical selections
  5. Develop a sufficiently detailed scope for pricing
  6. Compare bids, exclusions, assumptions and proposed schedules
  7. Finalize the contract and responsibility matrix
  8. Obtain required permits and approvals
  9. Order long-lead products and confirm delivery logic
  10. Plan the temporary kitchen and occupied-home protections
  11. Begin construction only when the project is ready
  12. Track decisions, changes, inspections and critical-path items through closeout

Important Note

Kitchen-remodel requirements and timelines vary by jurisdiction, property, building type, contractor, product and scope. This article provides general educational guidance and is not a project schedule, code determination, engineering opinion or guarantee. Confirm current requirements and project-specific timing with the qualified professionals responsible for the work.
