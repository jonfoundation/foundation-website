Living During Construction

Should You Live at Home During a Whole-Home Renovation?

The right living arrangement depends on project scope, essential services, safety, schedule, family needs and the true cost of disruption.

By Jon Lourash · Foundation Renovation Coaching

Published September 23, 2026

Why This Decision Matters

One of the most overlooked decisions in a whole-home renovation is where you will live during construction. Remaining in the home can reduce temporary housing costs, but it may also extend the schedule, increase stress, and complicate construction. Moving out often improves efficiency but comes with its own financial and logistical considerations.

When Staying in the Home May Be Appropriate

  • The renovation is confined to a small portion of the home.
  • Essential services such as a kitchen and at least one bathroom remain functional.
  • Dust, noise, and access can be reasonably managed.
  • There are no major health or safety concerns.
  • The contractor can separate the work area from occupied spaces.

When Moving Out Is Often the Better Choice

  • Most of the home will be under construction.
  • The kitchen and bathrooms will be unusable.
  • Major structural, electrical, plumbing, or HVAC work is planned.
  • Children, elderly family members, or pets would be affected by construction.
  • The project requires extensive demolition or hazardous material remediation.

Benefits of Moving Out

  • Construction can often progress more efficiently.
  • Contractors have unrestricted access to the work area.
  • Less daily disruption for your family.
  • Reduced exposure to dust, noise, and safety hazards.
  • Fewer delays caused by coordinating around occupied spaces.

Costs to Consider

Temporary housing is an additional project expense, but it should be weighed against the potential cost of a longer construction schedule, repeated setup and cleanup, and homeowner inconvenience.

  • Short-term rental or hotel costs
  • Storage expenses
  • Moving costs
  • Pet boarding or care
  • Additional commuting expenses

Coach's Perspective

Homeowners often focus on the monthly cost of temporary housing, but they rarely calculate the cost of living through a major renovation. Daily setup and cleanup, restricted work hours, protecting occupied spaces, maintaining a temporary kitchen, and coordinating construction around your family's schedule can all reduce productivity and extend the project. If your budget allows, moving out during a whole-home renovation is often an investment in a smoother, safer, and more efficient project—not simply an added expense.

Questions to Ask Yourself

  • Will I have access to a functioning kitchen and bathroom?
  • Can my family tolerate months of dust and noise?
  • Would moving out shorten the construction schedule?
  • How will construction affect children or pets?
  • Does the added cost of temporary housing provide better overall value?

Common Misconceptions

Staying in the home always saves money. Temporary housing adds cost, but a faster and more efficient project may offset part of that expense.

Contractors can easily work around us. Occupied homes require additional coordination, protection, and scheduling that can reduce efficiency.

Dust can be completely contained. Even with excellent protection, some dust and disruption should be expected during major renovations.

Moving out is only necessary for luxury projects. The decision should be based on project scope, safety, and practicality—not project cost.

Foundation Tip

Don't base this decision solely on the cost of temporary housing. Consider the value of a shorter construction schedule, improved contractor efficiency, reduced stress, and a safer environment for your family.

Closing Thought

Every renovation is different. By honestly evaluating your project's scope, your family's needs, and the practical realities of construction, you can choose the living arrangement that best supports a successful renovation and a smoother overall experience.
