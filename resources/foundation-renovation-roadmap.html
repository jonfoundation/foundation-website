A Renovation Is More Than the Construction Phase

Homeowners often think of a renovation as the period when contractors are working in the house. Construction is only one part of the journey. The decisions made before demolition—and the follow-through after the visible work is complete—often determine whether the project remains aligned with the homeowner’s goals, budget and expectations. The Foundation Renovation Roadmap™ organizes the complete journey into six phases:

  1. Discover
  2. Plan
  3. Design
  4. Prepare
  5. Build
  6. Complete

The Roadmap is not a rigid checklist, construction schedule or required project-delivery method. It is a decision framework. The phases help homeowners understand where they are, what information should exist, which decisions are approaching and what needs to be resolved before the next major commitment. Different phases may overlap. A budget may be revised during design, a product decision may affect planning, and an existing condition may require the team to reconsider an earlier assumption. The objective is not perfect linearity. It is to prevent important decisions from disappearing between participants or being delayed until they are expensive to change.

The Roadmap Is Also a Foundation Deliverable

Foundation uses the six-phase framework in its educational content and homeowner services. Following a Foundation Strategy Session™, the homeowner receives a written Foundation Renovation Roadmap™ that summarizes the project goals, priorities, apparent risks, budget considerations, unanswered questions, responsibilities and recommended next steps discussed during the session. The written Roadmap is specific to the homeowner’s situation, but it is not an architectural plan, engineering report, construction estimate or contractor scope of work. It is designed to organize the owner’s decisions and provide a clearer foundation for conversations with the professionals responsible for design, approvals and construction.

Phase 1: Discover

Discover begins with the reason for the renovation. Before defining rooms, products or a preferred solution, identify the problems the project should solve and the outcomes that would make it successful. Questions may include:

  • What is not working in the home today?
  • How do household routines, accessibility needs or future plans affect the project?
  • Which improvements would have the greatest effect on daily life?
  • How long does the homeowner expect to remain in the property?
  • Why does the project make sense now?
  • What assumptions are already shaping the desired solution?

The Discover phase should produce a clear problem statement and an initial set of priorities. This keeps the project focused when design options, costs and compromises appear later.

How Foundation Can Help During Discover

A complimentary Discovery Call helps determine whether Foundation is a useful fit. When more structured guidance is appropriate, the Strategy Session helps the homeowner clarify the project and identify the decisions that should be organized in the written Renovation Roadmap.

Phase 2: Plan

Plan translates the project goals into an initial strategy. The homeowner begins defining scope, budget, priorities, constraints, team needs, likely approvals and the information required before design or construction commitments are made. Planning should address:

  • The spaces, structures and systems likely to be affected
  • Must-haves, high-value improvements and optional features
  • The complete project budget—not only the construction contract
  • Existing-condition, site, utility or jurisdictional questions
  • Likely design, engineering and specialty-professional needs
  • Potential phasing or temporary-living considerations
  • The decisions that must be made before reliable pricing is possible

The Plan phase does not need to answer every design question. It should define the project well enough that the homeowner can engage the appropriate professionals with clearer goals and fewer hidden assumptions.

Where the Foundation Feasibility Report fits

For an ADU or home addition, the optional Foundation Feasibility Report™ can support the Discover and Plan phases by organizing available property and jurisdiction information, apparent site and existing-condition constraints, utility questions, likely follow-up investigations and recommended next steps. The Feasibility Report is separate from the Renovation Roadmap. The Roadmap organizes the homeowner’s overall project strategy and decisions; the Report focuses on preliminary property-specific due diligence for an ADU or addition. Neither replaces architectural, engineering, surveying, legal, code or agency responsibilities.

Phase 3: Design

Design turns the goals and planning assumptions into a coordinated, buildable solution. Depending on the project, this work may involve an architect, residential designer, interior designer, engineer, design-build firm, contractor or specialty consultants. During Design, the homeowner and project team should continue testing decisions against:

  • The original goals and priorities
  • The complete project budget
  • Existing conditions and professional investigations
  • Structural and building-system coordination
  • Permit and jurisdictional requirements
  • Product dimensions, performance and lead times
  • Constructability, sequencing and household disruption

Design is not only a visual exercise. Layout, structure, utilities, cabinetry, appliances, lighting, waterproofing, drainage and other technical elements must work together. Decisions made in isolation can create conflicts that appear during pricing or construction.

Phase 4: Prepare

Prepare is the bridge between design and construction. The goal is to confirm that the project is sufficiently defined, contracted and organized before demolition begins. Preparation commonly includes:

  • Completing permit documents and required approvals
  • Finalizing schedule-sensitive selections
  • Obtaining and comparing contractor proposals
  • Clarifying scope, allowances, exclusions and owner-supplied items
  • Reviewing the construction agreement and change procedures
  • Confirming project contacts, communication and decision responsibilities
  • Planning access, temporary protection, storage and household logistics
  • Updating the complete project budget and contingency

A project may not be completely free of uncertainty before construction, but the remaining unknowns should be visible, assigned and reflected in the budget and decision process.

Foundation Bid Review

Foundation Bid Review can help the homeowner compare proposal scope, allowances, exclusions, assumptions and responsibilities before selecting a contractor. The service helps identify questions and differences; it does not select the contractor, negotiate on the homeowner’s behalf, provide legal review or guarantee pricing or performance.

Phase 5: Build

Build is where the project documents and agreements are carried into construction. The contractor manages construction according to the contract, while the homeowner continues to make decisions, review changes, maintain records and communicate with the project team. Homeowners should understand:

  • Who communicates progress, schedule changes and field questions
  • How submittals, selections and owner decisions are tracked
  • How change orders are documented, priced and approved
  • How unexpected conditions will be investigated
  • Which inspections and professional observations are required
  • How payments and project records are organized
  • When the homeowner should raise a concern

The homeowner should remain informed without taking over the contractor’s responsibility to supervise trades, control the jobsite or determine construction means and methods.

Foundation Construction Support

Foundation Construction Support can help the homeowner prepare for project meetings, understand what information is missing, organize change questions and track decision responsibilities. Foundation does not supervise construction, direct trades, perform code inspections or replace architectural, engineering, legal or contractor responsibilities.

Phase 6: Complete

A project is not complete simply because the main construction activity has stopped. The Complete phase confirms that remaining work, documentation and owner responsibilities are addressed. Completion may include:

  • Final walkthroughs and punch-list documentation
  • Required inspections, approvals and occupancy documentation
  • Demonstrations and operating instructions
  • Warranties, manuals, finish records and product information
  • Final lien, payment and contract documentation as applicable
  • Cleaning, move-in and restoration
  • Maintenance planning and post-completion follow-up

Homeowners should understand what remains open, who is responsible and what documentation confirms completion.

Foundation Final Walk

Foundation Final Walk helps the homeowner prepare for and conduct a systematic review near project completion. It is a homeowner-support service—not a code inspection, professional certification, warranty determination or substitute for the contractor’s and design professionals’ closeout responsibilities.

What the Roadmap Helps Prevent

The six-phase framework is intended to reduce common process problems such as:

  • Beginning design before the goals and budget are understood
  • Requesting contractor pricing before the scope is comparable
  • Selecting products before their technical relationships are known
  • Leaving responsibility for permits, consultants or decisions unclear
  • Treating construction as the only part of the schedule
  • Using contingency for upgrades before project risks are understood
  • Entering construction with major selections or agreements unresolved
  • Treating substantial completion as the end of closeout

The Roadmap cannot eliminate uncertainty. It makes the uncertainty easier to see, investigate and assign before it becomes an urgent field decision.

How to Use the Roadmap With Your Project Team

The homeowner can use the framework to ask stage-appropriate questions:

  • Discover: Are we solving the right problem?
  • Plan: Are the scope, budget and constraints sufficiently understood?
  • Design: Are the decisions coordinated and buildable?
  • Prepare: Are the documents, pricing, agreements and logistics ready?
  • Build: Are changes, decisions and responsibilities being documented?
  • Complete: Is the project, paperwork and owner handoff actually finished?

Each professional remains responsible for their contracted scope. The Roadmap helps the homeowner understand how those scopes connect and when missing information could affect the next commitment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does every renovation follow these phases?

Most renovations address each category in some form, although the amount of work and the overlap between phases vary. A cosmetic project may move quickly, while an addition, ADU or whole-home renovation may require extensive feasibility, design, approvals and preconstruction.

Is the Foundation Renovation Roadmap an architectural plan?

No. It is an owner-focused planning and decision document. It does not provide drawings, engineering, specifications, permits or contractor pricing.

Can I use the Roadmap with a design-build firm?

Yes. The framework does not require a particular delivery method. It can help a homeowner understand decisions and responsibilities in an architect-led, design-build, contractor-led or independently assembled team.

Do I need every Foundation service?

No. Services are selected according to the homeowner’s project and decision point. Some homeowners need only a Strategy Session and written Roadmap; others may later use Bid Review, Construction Support or Final Walk. The Feasibility Report is an optional add-on intended primarily for ADUs and home additions.

Foundation Tip

Do not judge project progress only by how quickly construction can start. Progress also means replacing assumptions with decisions, assigning responsibilities and creating information the next phase can rely on.

Important Note
