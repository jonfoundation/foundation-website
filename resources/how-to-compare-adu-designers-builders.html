How to Compare ADU Designers and Builders

Compare ADU designers and builders using consistent scope, deliverables, assumptions, exclusions, credentials and responsibilities.

By Jon Lourash · Foundation Renovation Coaching

Published August 26, 2026 · Updated September 20, 2026

Choosing an ADU designer or builder is difficult because proposals that look similar on the first page may include very different services, assumptions and risks. One firm may quote design only. Another may include engineering and permit coordination. A builder may include utility connections but exclude trenching, finish materials or site restoration.

The right comparison is not simply “Who has the lowest fee?” It is “Which team is offering the clearest, most complete path for this property and this project?”

This guide explains how to compare ADU designers and builders on a consistent basis before you sign a design agreement or construction contract.

Start by identifying the delivery model

Before comparing firms, understand how each one expects the project team to be organized.

Separate designer and builder

An architect or residential designer prepares the design and permit documents. A contractor is selected separately to price and construct the ADU. This model can give the homeowner an independent design advocate and more flexibility in contractor selection, but it requires clear coordination between design and construction.

Design-build

One company is responsible for both design and construction, either with in-house staff or outside consultants. This can simplify communication, but the homeowner should still identify who performs each service, when the construction price becomes reliable and what happens if the project does not proceed to construction.

Prefabricated or modular provider

A provider may supply a standardized building package, while site design, foundations, utility work, transportation, installation and permits are handled separately or through partners. Confirm the system’s construction classification, valid factory approvals and remaining property-specific requirements with the applicable agencies. See Prefabricated vs. Site-Built ADUs.

None of these models is automatically best. The important question is whether responsibilities, deliverables and decision points are clearly defined.

Compare the same project—not just the same square footage

A fair comparison begins with a shared baseline. Give each candidate the same available information and ask each to state the assumptions behind the proposal.

At minimum, align:

Property address and known site constraints

Approximate ADU size, location and intended use

Attached, detached, conversion or prefabricated concept

Expected finish level

Known utility conditions

Available survey, title, soils or existing-plan information

Target schedule and budget range

Services the homeowner expects the firm to provide

If one proposal assumes a flat, accessible site with nearby utilities and another includes difficult access, utility upgrades or retaining work, their prices are not measuring the same project.

How to compare ADU designers

A designer’s fee should be evaluated against the work product and level of involvement—not just the number at the bottom of the proposal.

Confirm the design scope

Ask whether the fee includes:

Initial site and zoning review

Existing-condition documentation

Concept or schematic design

Design revisions and the number of included rounds

Permit or construction documents

Structural, civil, energy or other engineering

Survey coordination

Planning or zoning applications

Permit submittal and plan-check responses

Coordination with the builder or prefabricated provider

Construction-phase questions, site visits or submittal review

A proposal that ends at permit issuance is different from one that supports the homeowner through construction.

Understand who will do the work

Ask who will be the primary contact, who will prepare the drawings and which consultants will be hired separately. Review the experience of the people assigned to your project, not only the firm’s portfolio.

Licensing rules for architects, engineers, designers and contractors vary by state and locality. Verify required credentials with the appropriate licensing or registration board. A professional title, attractive website or inclusion in a referral list is not a substitute for that check.

Evaluate relevant experience

Look for experience with projects that resemble yours in:

ADU type and construction method

Lot size, access and topography

Historic, coastal, wildfire, flood or other special review areas

Utility constraints

Local review procedures

Budget and finish level

Local permitting experience can be useful, but it should not replace sound documentation, communication and project-management practices.

How to compare ADU builders

Construction proposals are most useful when they are based on the same drawings, specifications and finish assumptions. Early budgets based on concepts can still be valuable, but they should be labeled as preliminary and include a clear list of assumptions.

Normalize the construction scope

Compare whether each proposal includes:

Demolition, clearing and site protection

Excavation, grading, foundations and drainage

Retaining walls or unusual soil conditions

Utility trenching, connections, meters and upgrades

Structure, roofing, windows and exterior finishes

Heating, cooling, plumbing and electrical systems

Interior finishes, fixtures, appliances and cabinets

Permit fees, impact fees or utility charges

Temporary facilities, supervision and general conditions

Testing, inspections and closeout

Landscaping, fencing, paving and site restoration

Sales tax, delivery, freight or crane costs where applicable

Do not assume that a line item is included because it is necessary to complete the project.

Review allowances and exclusions

An allowance is a placeholder, not a guaranteed final cost. Ask what quantity and quality level it assumes, whether labor and tax are included, and how overages or credits will be calculated.

Exclusions deserve equal attention. A lower proposal may leave survey work, engineering, utility upgrades, finish selections or permit coordination to the homeowner. Those costs and responsibilities do not disappear; they move outside the quoted price.

Compare schedule and change procedures

Ask when the builder could start, how long the work is expected to take and what conditions could alter the schedule. Review how changes are documented, priced and approved. A clear change-order process is more valuable than a promise that changes will not happen.

Check credentials, insurance and history

Verify the contractor’s license or registration where required, along with bond and insurance information available through the relevant state or local authority. Ask for proof of applicable insurance and confirm who is responsible for subcontractors.

For example, the California Contractors State License Board recommends comparing written bids based on the same plans, specifications and scope. Check credentials with the authority for your own jurisdiction. The lowest bid should not be selected automatically when scope, quality or qualifications differ.

Use a scope matrix

A simple comparison matrix can expose differences that proposal totals hide. Put candidates across the top and the following categories down the side:

Design phases and deliverables

Consultants and engineering

Permit preparation and agency responses

Preconstruction and estimating

Site work and utility work

Materials, fixtures and allowances

Owner responsibilities

Exclusions

Construction support

Schedule assumptions

Payment terms

Change-order process

Warranty and closeout

For each category, mark Included, Excluded, Allowance, Optional or Unclear. Resolve “Unclear” items in writing before making a selection.

Questions to ask an ADU designer

Who will work on my project day to day?

What site and code research is included before design begins?

How many design options and revision rounds are included?

Which consultants are included, and which will I hire separately?

Who submits for permits and responds to agency comments?

How will budget feedback be incorporated during design?

What support is included while I select a builder?

What services continue during construction?

What similar projects have you completed recently?

May I speak with recent clients?

Questions to ask an ADU builder

Is this a preliminary estimate, a fixed price or another pricing format?

Which drawings, specifications and assumptions support the price?

What site work and utility work are included?

Which materials are allowances, and how were the amounts established?

What is specifically excluded?

Who will supervise the site and communicate with me?

How are substitutions and change orders handled?

What schedule assumptions are built into the proposal?

What warranty and closeout documentation are provided?

May I speak with recent clients and visit a completed project?

Red flags to investigate

Proceed carefully when a firm:

Will not provide a written scope

Pressures you to sign before questions are answered

Uses a price that is dramatically different without explaining why

Avoids discussing exclusions, allowances or change orders

Cannot identify who will perform the work

Will not provide recent references

Asks you to rely on verbal promises

Uses credentials that cannot be verified

Promises permit approval or a construction schedule that no one can control

Requires payment terms that conflict with applicable consumer-protection rules

A red flag is a reason to investigate, not necessarily proof that a firm is unsuitable.
