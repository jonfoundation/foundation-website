Construction Contract Types

Fixed Price vs. Cost Plus Contracts

Compare how fixed-price and cost-plus contracts handle scope definition, cost visibility, flexibility and risk before construction begins.

By Jon Lourash · Foundation Renovation Coaching

Published September 21, 2026

One of the most important decisions during a renovation is how your construction contract will be structured. Two of the most common contract types are Fixed Price and Cost Plus. Both are widely used in residential construction, and neither is inherently better. The right choice depends on the project's level of definition, the homeowner's priorities, and how both parties prefer to manage risk.

In a Fixed Price contract, the contractor agrees to complete a defined scope of work for an agreed-upon price. This provides homeowners with greater cost certainty, assuming the project scope remains unchanged. If the homeowner changes the scope or unforeseen conditions require additional work, those changes are typically addressed through change orders.

In a Cost Plus contract, the homeowner pays the actual cost of labor, materials, subcontractors, and other project expenses, plus an agreed-upon fee or percentage for the contractor's services. This approach offers greater transparency into project costs and can provide flexibility when the design is still evolving or the scope cannot be fully defined before construction begins.

Neither contract eliminates risk. Instead, each allocates risk differently. Fixed Price contracts generally place more pricing risk on the contractor, while Cost Plus contracts often place more cost variability on the homeowner. Understanding that difference is more important than deciding which contract is 'better.'

Homeowners sometimes assume a Fixed Price contract guarantees there will never be additional costs. In reality, changes in scope, hidden conditions, owner-requested upgrades, or unforeseen site conditions can still affect the final cost under either contract type.

Likewise, Cost Plus contracts are sometimes viewed as unpredictable. While costs can vary, many include detailed budgets, regular reporting, agreed-upon markups, and approval processes that help homeowners stay informed throughout construction.

Fixed Price May Be a Good Fit If You...

  • Have a well-defined design and scope of work.
  • Value greater budget certainty before construction begins.
  • Prefer fewer pricing variables during construction.
  • Are comfortable making most selections before work starts.

Cost Plus May Be a Good Fit If You...

  • Expect the design to evolve during construction.
  • Are renovating an older home with greater uncertainty.
  • Want detailed visibility into project costs.
  • Value flexibility as decisions are made.

Common Misconceptions

  • Fixed Price means the final cost can never change. Changes in scope, unforeseen conditions, and owner-requested modifications are typically handled through change orders.
  • Cost Plus means the contractor has no incentive to control costs. Reputable contractors rely on trust, communication, and efficient project management regardless of contract type.
  • One contract type is always better. Each has strengths and tradeoffs depending on the project and the homeowner's goals.
  • Overhead and profit are unnecessary add-ons. Every contractor has real business costs associated with planning, supervision, insurance, administration, and warranty support. These costs are simply reflected differently depending on the contract structure.
  • Cost Plus is only for large luxury homes. While common on complex projects, Cost Plus can also be appropriate for renovations where the scope cannot be fully defined before work begins.

Foundation Tip

Rather than asking which contract type is better, ask which one best matches your project's level of definition, your tolerance for risk, and how involved you want to be in financial decision-making during construction.

Closing Thought

A construction contract is more than a pricing method—it establishes how the project will be managed, how changes will be handled, and how risk is shared between the homeowner and the contractor. Understanding both approaches helps you choose the agreement that best supports a successful renovation.
