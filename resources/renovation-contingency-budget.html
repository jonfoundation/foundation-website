How Much Contingency Should You Budget for a Renovation?

Set a project-specific reserve by considering existing conditions, scope, documentation, unresolved decisions, and the risks that remain.

Contingency Is a Plan for Uncertainty

A renovation budget should account for more than the work you expect to complete. It should also include money reserved for conditions, requirements or scope adjustments that could not be fully defined when the budget was prepared.

That reserve is commonly called contingency. It is not an invitation to spend more, and it is not the same as a finish allowance or a wish-list fund. It is a deliberate financial buffer that helps homeowners respond to legitimate project uncertainty without immediately sacrificing priorities or stopping construction.

Good planning can reduce uncertainty, but it cannot remove every unknown from work involving an existing home. The goal is not to predict every surprise. The goal is to understand where uncertainty remains and reserve an amount that reflects the project’s actual risk.

How Much Should You Set Aside?

There is no single percentage that is right for every renovation. As a general planning starting point, a limited and well-defined project may use a smaller reserve, while an invasive renovation, addition or project involving an older or poorly documented home may require a larger one.

Homeowners often begin discussions in these broad ranges:

  • About 5% to 10% for limited work with complete documents, known conditions and relatively little demolition.

  • About 10% to 15% for a typical renovation with some concealed conditions, coordination risk or decisions still being finalized.

  • About 15% to 20% or more for older homes, major structural work, additions, extensive demolition, uncertain utilities or projects with significant unresolved conditions.

These are planning ranges, not universal rules or guarantees. A percentage should never replace a project-specific review. A smaller project can still contain a costly unknown, and a large project with strong documentation may carry less risk than its size suggests. Your contractor, designer and financial adviser should help you determine an appropriate reserve for your circumstances.

What Makes a Project Need More Contingency?

Contingency should generally increase when the project depends on assumptions that have not yet been verified. Common risk factors include:

  • An older home or limited records of previous renovations

  • Walls, ceilings, floors or foundations that will be opened or altered

  • Structural modifications, additions or significant changes to the building envelope

  • Unknown soil, drainage, grading or foundation conditions

  • Aging or undersized electrical, plumbing, sewer, water, gas or mechanical systems

  • Incomplete drawings, engineering, selections or finish specifications

  • Unresolved zoning, permit, utility or jurisdictional requirements

  • Limited access, occupied construction, phasing or difficult site logistics

  • Long-lead products, price-sensitive materials or items without confirmed availability

  • A broad scope that must fit within a fixed maximum budget

Several smaller uncertainties can compound. For example, an addition may involve structural design, utility capacity, foundation conditions, drainage and permit review. Even when no single issue appears critical, the combination may justify a more conservative reserve.

What Can Reduce Uncertainty?

The most useful way to manage contingency is to reduce avoidable unknowns before construction and then reserve funds for the risk that remains. Helpful early work may include:

  • Documenting existing conditions and reviewing available records

  • Completing surveys, testing, engineering or other targeted investigation

  • Coordinating architectural, structural and building-system drawings

  • Making major selections before bids are finalized

  • Defining contractor scope, allowances, exclusions and owner-provided items

  • Obtaining early input on access, sequencing, temporary protection and site logistics

  • Confirming permit, utility and jurisdictional questions as early as practical

Investigation can convert an unknown into a defined scope item. That often improves the budget, even when the investigation uncovers work that must be added. Finding a requirement before construction is usually easier to plan around than discovering it after demolition.

Early investigation does not eliminate contingency. Some existing conditions remain concealed, and agencies, utilities or field conditions may introduce requirements that could not reasonably be confirmed earlier.

How the Foundation Feasibility Report Can Help

For a home addition or ADU, the Foundation Feasibility Report™ add-on can help organize the early property and project questions that influence budget risk. Depending on the project, the report may identify planning issues related to site constraints, zoning, access, the existing structure, utilities, drainage, foundations and other conditions that deserve follow-up.

The report is intended to help homeowners see where uncertainty exists and what additional information may be needed from an architect, engineer, surveyor, soils professional, contractor, jurisdiction or utility provider. It can make contingency planning more informed by separating known constraints from questions that still require investigation.

A feasibility review cannot guarantee that construction will have no surprises, and it does not replace licensed design, engineering, inspection, code, legal or contractor responsibilities. Its value is in helping the homeowner investigate the right issues earlier, before assumptions become expensive commitments.

Contingency Is Not the Same as an Allowance

Several budget terms are often treated as interchangeable even though they serve different purposes:

  • Contingency: money reserved for uncertainty or legitimate unforeseen conditions.

  • Allowance: a placeholder for a known item whose exact product, quantity or price has not been finalized.

  • Alternate: optional work priced separately so it can be added or removed deliberately.

  • Escalation: an allowance for possible price increases over time, especially when construction will not begin soon.

  • Change order: the written modification used to adjust the contract scope, price or time after the agreement is signed.

A low allowance can create an overage, but that does not automatically make the overage a contingency expense. Likewise, an owner-requested upgrade is usually a scope decision, not an unforeseen condition. Keeping these categories separate makes the budget easier to understand and manage.

Who Controls the Contingency?

The homeowner’s project budget should clearly show where the reserve is held and who may authorize its use. A contractor may also carry an internal contingency within a proposal or contract, but homeowners should not assume that one exists or that it covers every type of risk. Ask what is included, what is excluded and how unused amounts are handled.

A practical contingency plan should:

  • Identify the project’s most important unresolved conditions.

  • Assign responsibility for additional investigation or decisions.

  • Establish a reserve that reflects the remaining risk and the homeowner’s financial tolerance.

  • Define who can approve use of the reserve.

  • Track each use separately from allowances, upgrades and ordinary contract scope.

  • Reforecast the remaining budget as the project progresses.

Avoid treating unused contingency as available upgrade money too early. A project may pass demolition successfully and still face permit, utility, delivery, installation or closeout issues later.

When Is It Reasonable to Use Contingency?

Legitimate uses may include concealed deterioration, undocumented prior work, existing systems that cannot support the approved design, field conditions that differ materially from available information, or a requirement that could not reasonably be confirmed before construction.

Contingency should not automatically pay for incomplete contractor scope, duplicated charges, voluntary upgrades or work that was already included in the contract. Before approving a cost, confirm what changed, why it was not included, who is responsible, whether alternatives exist, and how the decision affects schedule and remaining reserve.

Questions to Ask Before Finalizing Your Reserve

  • Which parts of the scope depend on assumptions rather than verified information?

  • What conditions will remain concealed until demolition?

  • Which selections, allowances or design decisions are still open?

  • Does the contractor’s price contain a contingency, and exactly what does it cover?

  • What investigations could reduce a major unknown before construction?

  • Can the project continue if the reserve is partly or fully used?

  • How will contingency decisions be documented and tracked?

Foundation Tip

Do not choose a contingency percentage first and then assume the project fits it. Start by identifying the unknowns, reduce the ones you reasonably can, and size the reserve around the risk that remains.

Important Note

This article provides general educational information. Renovation costs, concealed conditions, contract terms and permit or utility requirements vary by property, project and jurisdiction. Consult the appropriate licensed professionals and financial advisers before establishing or using a project contingency.
