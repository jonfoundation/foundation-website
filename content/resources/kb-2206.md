<section id="bid-answer">

<h2>How Do You Compare Contractor Bids?</h2>
<p>Start by comparing scope, not price. Two competent contractors can submit very different totals because their proposals may not include the same work, materials, allowances, assumptions, schedule expectations, or level of project management.</p>
<p>Before deciding that one contractor is expensive or another is a bargain, determine whether they are pricing the same renovation. A useful comparison asks three questions: What is included? What is excluded or still uncertain? What will the homeowner likely be responsible for once those differences are understood?</p>
<p>The goal is not to force every proposal into the same format. It is to make the differences visible enough to support an informed decision.</p>
</section>

<section id="same-scope">

<h2>1. Confirm That Everyone Is Bidding the Same Information</h2>
<p>A fair comparison begins before the proposals arrive. Give each bidder the same available drawings, specifications, finish information, known site conditions, requested alternates, and schedule expectations. If the design is incomplete, identify what remains unresolved so bidders do not fill the gaps with unrelated assumptions.</p>
<p>Confirm which document issue or revision each contractor used. A proposal based on an earlier plan set may omit work that appears in the current documents.</p>
</section>

<section id="2-align-the-scope-of-work">

<h2>2. Align the Scope of Work</h2>
<p>If one proposal includes work that another does not, the totals are not directly comparable. Review major categories such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>Demolition, protection, hauling, and disposal</li>
<li>Framing, structural work, and repairs to existing conditions</li>
<li>Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and utility work</li>
<li>Waterproofing, drywall, tile, flooring, cabinetry, countertops, and painting</li>
<li>Fixtures, appliances, finish materials, and installation</li>
<li>Permits, inspections, engineering, testing, and other professional services</li>
<li>Site access, temporary facilities, cleanup, punch-list work, and closeout records</li>
</ul>
<p>A missing item can make a proposal appear artificially low without implying dishonesty. The bidder may have interpreted the documents differently or assumed another party would perform the work. Ask for written clarification.</p>
</section>

<section id="allowances">

<h2>3. Compare Allowances Carefully</h2>
<p>Allowances are placeholders for items not fully selected or priced. One contractor may carry an entry-level tile allowance while another assumes a higher-end product, making the second proposal look more expensive even when labor and markup are similar.</p>
<ul>
<li>What product, quantity, and quality level does the allowance assume?</li>
<li>Does it cover material, labor, or both?</li>
<li>Are tax, freight, delivery, waste, installation, and contractor markup included?</li>
<li>What happens when the actual cost is higher or lower?</li>
<li>Does the allowance reasonably match the products you are considering?</li>
</ul>
<p>The more selections that are defined before final pricing, the more reliable the comparison becomes.</p>
</section>

<section id="exclusions">

<h2>4. Identify Exclusions, Qualifications, and Owner-Supplied Items</h2>
<p>Exclusions and qualifications are as important as inclusions. A lower total may shift costs or responsibilities to the homeowner. Look for painting, appliances, fixtures, landscaping, utility upgrades, hazardous-material work, permit fees, engineering, storage, moving, temporary housing, and final cleaning, as applicable.</p>
<p>Do not assume an item is included merely because it appears on the drawings. Confirm important responsibilities in writing before relying on them in the comparison.</p>
</section>

<section id="5-review-assumptions-and-unknown-conditions">

<h2>5. Review Assumptions and Unknown Conditions</h2>
<p>Proposals may assume normal working hours, clear access, available parking, adequate utilities, no hazardous materials, reusable existing systems, or limited repair of concealed conditions. These assumptions can affect price and should be evaluated against the actual property.</p>
<p>No contractor can fully price every concealed condition before demolition. The important distinction is whether known risks are addressed, unknowns are clearly identified, and the contract explains how additional work will be documented and authorized.</p>
</section>

<section id="normalize">

<h2>6. Normalize the Proposals Before Comparing Totals</h2>
<p>Create a comparison worksheet with major scope categories in rows and contractors in columns. Mark each item as included, excluded, allowance, alternate, owner-provided, or unclear. Then obtain clarifications for material gaps.</p>
<p>Avoid adding unsupported guesses to a bid. If Contractor A excludes work included by Contractor B, ask Contractor A to provide a price or written clarification. The objective is not to manufacture identical totals; it is to understand what each total represents.</p>
</section>

<section id="7-evaluate-schedule-and-staffing">

<h2>7. Evaluate Schedule and Staffing</h2>
<p>A lower price may be paired with a longer schedule, fewer dedicated staff, or different supervision. Compare the anticipated start, duration, major milestones, work hours, procurement assumptions, and responsibility for schedule updates.</p>
<p>Ask who will manage the project, who will supervise the site, how many other projects those people will handle, and how frequently the homeowner will receive updates.</p>
</section>

<section id="price-detail">

<h2>8. Review Commercial and Change-Order Terms</h2>
<p>Proposal comparison should include the payment structure and the process for changes—not only the initial contract amount.</p>
<ul>
<li>Deposit and progress-payment schedule</li>
<li>Allowances, alternates, and unit prices</li>
<li>Contractor markup on changes, allowances, or owner-requested work</li>
<li>Documentation and approval required before changed work proceeds</li>
<li>Schedule extensions and delay responsibilities</li>
<li>Warranty, punch-list, and closeout obligations</li>
</ul>
<p>Contract terms may have legal and financial consequences. Consult a qualified attorney or other appropriate professional when advice is needed.</p>
</section>

<section id="more-than-cost">

<h2>9. Compare More Than Construction Cost</h2>
<p>A remodeling contractor also provides management. Consider communication, supervision, documentation, scheduling, quality-control processes, problem-solving, warranty support, and confidence in the assigned team. These services have real value even when they are not separate line items.</p>
<p>The lowest bid may still be the best choice, but it should be selected because it represents the best overall fit and value—not simply because the bottom-line number is smallest.</p>
</section>

<section id="differences">

<h2>Why Are Contractor Bids So Different?</h2>
<p>Differences may come from scope, material assumptions, labor and subcontractor pricing, overhead, insurance, staffing, schedule, risk, markup, or interpretation of the documents. A large spread does not prove that the high bidder is overcharging or the low bidder missed something. Treat the spread as a signal that further review is needed.</p>
</section>

<section id="high-low">

<h2>Should You Automatically Eliminate the Highest or Lowest Bid?</h2>
<p>No. A low bid deserves additional review when it contains less detail, low allowances, important exclusions, or scope that does not align with the documents. A high bid deserves the same scrutiny to determine whether it includes additional work, higher specifications, more management, a different approach, or simply higher pricing.</p>
<p>The objective is not to make every bid equal. It is to understand why they differ.</p>
</section>

<section id="questions">

<h2>Questions to Ask Before You Decide</h2>
<ul>
<li>Can you confirm the exact scope included in this proposal?</li>
<li>Which documents and revisions did you use?</li>
<li>What are the most important exclusions, qualifications, and assumptions?</li>
<li>Which items are allowances, and how were the amounts established?</li>
<li>Are any items shown on the plans omitted from the price?</li>
<li>What could reasonably increase the contract amount?</li>
<li>How are change orders priced, documented, and approved?</li>
<li>What schedule and staffing assumptions support the proposal?</li>
<li>What warranty and closeout process are included?</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="mistakes">

<h2>Common Bid-Comparison Mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Comparing only total price instead of the underlying scope</li>
<li>Assuming the drawings guarantee that every contractor included every item</li>
<li>Ignoring allowances because they already appear in the total</li>
<li>Failing to account for exclusions and owner-supplied work</li>
<li>Treating every price difference as contractor markup</li>
<li>Relying on verbal promises instead of written clarification</li>
<li>Choosing a contractor before resolving major unanswered questions</li>
<li>Ignoring communication, management, schedule, and overall fit</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="closing-thought">

<h2>Closing Thought</h2>
<p>A good bid comparison does not reduce contractor selection to a spreadsheet. It makes the proposals understandable enough to evaluate price alongside scope, risk, management, schedule, communication, and fit. The clearer those differences are before signing, the fewer avoidable surprises the homeowner is likely to encounter later.</p>
</section>
