<section id="bid-answer"><h2>How Should You Compare Contractor Bids?</h2><p><strong>Compare renovation bids by first confirming that each contractor priced substantially the same project. Build a side-by-side comparison of scope, quantities, products, allowances, exclusions, owner-supplied items, existing-condition assumptions, schedule, supervision, payment structure, and change procedures. Ask contractors to clarify material differences in writing; only then compare price and overall value.</strong></p><p>Proposal totals can differ because contractors interpreted the documents differently, carried different products or services, used different allowances, or assigned risk differently. A low total may omit work; a high total may include work others excluded. Neither conclusion should be assumed without review.</p><p>This article organizes proposal content. To evaluate the companies and people behind the proposals, read <a href="/resources/how-to-choose-a-renovation-contractor.html">How to Choose the Right Renovation Contractor</a>. Keep each construction proposal in the complete project budget described in <a href="/resources/how-to-create-a-renovation-budget.html">How to Create a Realistic Renovation Budget</a>.</p><p>This is general homeowner education, not estimating, contracting, legal, financial, insurance, architectural, engineering, permitting, inspection, or safety advice. The proposal and eventual agreement control the parties' obligations; obtain qualified advice before relying on a general explanation.</p></section>

<div class="article-callout"><h3>A bid comparison organizes information; it does not choose the contractor</h3><p>The comparison can reveal differences and questions. It cannot guarantee that every condition is known, determine legal rights, validate technical adequacy, or predict contractor performance. Foundation does not select or endorse contractors.</p></div>

<section id="same-project"><h2>Confirm the Contractors Priced the Same Project</h2><p>Create a bid record listing exactly what each contractor received: drawing titles and dates, specifications, addenda, selection schedules, reports, site information, requested alternates, and the proposal due date. If bidders used different document versions, correct that before comparing totals.</p><p>Ask each contractor to identify documents relied upon and list assumptions made where information was incomplete. CSLB's <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Hire_A_Contractor/Finding_The_Right_Contractor.aspx">consumer hiring guidance</a> similarly advises giving bidders consistent plans and specifications and comparing written proposals.</p><p>If the project is not defined enough for comparable pricing, return to planning or design. <a href="/resources/how-to-plan-home-renovation.html">How to Plan a Home Renovation</a> explains the larger sequence, and the <a href="/resources/foundation-renovation-roadmap.html">Foundation Renovation Roadmap™</a> helps identify whether unresolved work belongs in Plan, Design, or Prepare.</p></section>

<section id="scope-comparison"><h2>Build a Side-by-Side Scope Comparison</h2><p>Break the work into meaningful categories rather than comparing only totals. The categories should reflect this project, but may include:</p><ul><li>Site protection, access, temporary facilities, and selective demolition.</li><li>Structural, framing, exterior-envelope, roofing, and site work.</li><li>Plumbing, mechanical, electrical, lighting, low-voltage, and fire-protection work.</li><li>Insulation, drywall, waterproofing, tile, cabinetry, counters, flooring, paint, and finish carpentry.</li><li>Appliances, fixtures, hardware, glazing, and specialty products.</li><li>Permits, required inspections, testing, debris removal, final cleaning, and closeout.</li></ul><p>For each row, record whether the item is included, excluded, allowed for, owner-supplied, priced as an alternate, or unclear. Add quantities, product levels, and labor assumptions where differences matter. Do not force unlike descriptions into a false match.</p><h3>Compare scope ownership</h3><p>Identify who furnishes, delivers, stores, installs, protects, connects, tests, and warrants each major item. “Owner supplied” may exclude more than purchase price. The homeowner's complete budget may need tax, shipping, storage, damage replacement, accessories, installation, and coordination.</p></section>

<section id="allowances"><h2>Separate Fixed Prices From Allowances</h2><p>An allowance is a placeholder for a defined item or category that is not fully selected or priced. It is not automatically a complete price for that portion of work.</p><p>For every allowance, ask:</p><ul><li>What products, quantities, quality assumptions, or unit prices form the amount?</li><li>Does it cover material only, or also labor, delivery, tax, setting materials, accessories, waste, supervision, and markup?</li><li>Who makes the selection and by what date?</li><li>How will actual cost above or below the allowance be calculated and documented?</li><li>Does the schedule assume a specific lead time or selection deadline?</li></ul><p>Normalize allowances cautiously. Replacing every contractor's figure with one common amount can make totals easier to view, but it does not erase differences in included labor, markup, procurement responsibility, or contract treatment.</p></section>

<section id="exclusions"><h2>Identify Exclusions and Owner-Supplied Items</h2><p>Read exclusions as carefully as inclusions. Some are explicit; others appear as qualifications, notes, “by owner” labels, or missing categories.</p><p>Common questions include:</p><ul><li>Who carries design revisions, engineering, surveys, testing, or agency fees?</li><li>Are permit fees included, and who is responsible for obtaining permits?</li><li>Who handles utility work, hazardous-material assessment or abatement, and concealed damage?</li><li>Are painting, patching, landscape restoration, protection, hauling, and final cleaning complete?</li><li>Which fixtures, appliances, hardware, or finishes are owner-purchased?</li><li>Who coordinates and warrants owner-supplied products?</li></ul><p>The omission of an item from a proposal does not prove it is required or unnecessary. Ask the appropriate designer, engineer, agency, safety professional, contractor, or legal adviser to resolve questions within their scope.</p></section>

<section id="assumptions"><h2>Compare Assumptions About Existing Conditions</h2><p>Renovations involve conditions that may not be visible before work begins. Proposals may assume existing framing is adequate, utilities have capacity, surfaces are level, access is available, or concealed materials are free of damage or hazardous substances.</p><p>Create an assumptions and unknowns list. For each item, record:</p><ul><li>What each contractor assumed.</li><li>What evidence supports the assumption.</li><li>Whether investigation can reduce uncertainty before signing.</li><li>Who evaluates the condition and within what professional scope.</li><li>How the proposal or agreement treats a different field condition.</li></ul><p>Do not ask a bid spreadsheet to resolve structural, environmental, code, or safety questions. Obtain qualified investigation and advice. Early cost context is also explained in <a href="/resources/how-much-does-a-home-renovation-cost.html">How Much Does a Home Renovation Really Cost?</a>.</p></section>

<section id="schedule-management"><h2>Review Schedule and Project-Management Commitments</h2><p>Compare the basis of each schedule, not just a proposed duration. Ask about prerequisites, permits, selections, procurement, mobilization, work sequencing, inspections, owner decisions, known nonworking periods, and how updates are issued.</p><p>Then identify services that may be embedded in the total:</p><ul><li>Named project manager and site supervision.</li><li>Meeting and reporting cadence.</li><li>Schedule updates and decision tracking.</li><li>Protection, access, dust-control coordination, and cleanup.</li><li>Subcontractor, delivery, design-team, and inspection coordination.</li><li>Punch, documentation, warranty, and closeout process.</li></ul><p>These descriptions help explain how a contractor plans to deliver the work. They are not guarantees. Ask for unclear commitments to be documented in the proposal and, if selected, the agreement.</p></section>

<section id="changes-payments"><h2>Understand Change-Order and Payment Provisions</h2><p>Proposal comparison should identify the proposed payment structure and change process, but legal evaluation belongs with qualified counsel.</p><p>CSLB's current <a href="https://cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Hire_A_Contractor/Home_Improvement_Contracts/What_Is_A_Contract.aspx">home-improvement contract guidance</a> explains California requirements for covered written contracts, detailed payment schedules, permit responsibility, signed written changes, and limits on down payments and progress payments, subject to stated exceptions. Do not copy a payment structure from another project or rely on this summary instead of the current law and actual agreement.</p><h3>Questions for each proposal</h3><ul><li>What deposit and progress-payment structure is proposed?</li><li>What work, materials, or milestone supports each payment?</li><li>How are additions, deductions, credits, and schedule effects documented?</li><li>Which markup, fee, or pricing method applies to changes?</li><li>Who may authorize a change, and must approval occur before work?</li><li>How are disputed or pending items tracked?</li></ul><p>Mechanics lien rights can involve parties the homeowner did not contract with directly. Review CSLB's <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/consumers/legal_issues_for_consumers/mechanics_lien/">mechanics lien guidance</a>, understand preliminary notices and lien releases, and obtain legal advice for the project. Foundation does not provide lien or contract advice.</p></section>

<section id="ambiguity"><h2>Identify Missing or Ambiguous Information</h2><p>Create an open-questions log rather than filling gaps with your own assumptions. Prioritize items that could materially affect price, schedule, quality, risk, or responsibility.</p><p>Examples include:</p><ul><li>A scope heading with no product, quantity, or installation description.</li><li>A permit line without fees or responsible party identified.</li><li>An allowance without labor or markup treatment.</li><li>“Standard” or “builder grade” without a defined reference.</li><li>Schedule language without start prerequisites or update process.</li><li>Owner-supplied products without delivery, storage, installation, or warranty responsibilities.</li><li>Warranty language that does not identify coverage, exclusions, or process.</li></ul><p>Use a status such as open, answered, proposal revised, agreement item, professional review, or homeowner decision. Preserve contractor responses and document versions.</p></section>

<section id="clarifications"><h2>Ask Contractors to Clarify Material Differences</h2><p>Send concise, neutral questions tied to specific proposal sections. Provide the same new project information to every active bidder when it changes the requested scope. Ask for revised proposal language or a written addendum where the answer affects the offer.</p><p>Useful prompts include:</p><ul><li>“Please confirm whether this line includes material, labor, tax, delivery, and installation.”</li><li>“Which drawing detail and product assumption support this amount?”</li><li>“Please identify who obtains the permit and whether agency fees are included.”</li><li>“What existing condition did you assume, and how would a different condition be addressed?”</li><li>“Please describe the supervision and schedule-update services included.”</li><li>“How would this allowance be adjusted after selection?”</li></ul><p>A large price difference does not prove one contractor is wrong. The clarification process often reveals a scope difference, risk assumption, service difference, mistake, or genuinely different approach.</p></section>

<section id="value"><h2>Compare Value Only After Normalizing the Scope</h2><p>Once material differences are visible, compare the packages as they actually stand. Keep original totals, then show adjustment notes separately; do not rewrite a contractor's offer as though your spreadsheet were the proposal.</p><p>Consider:</p><ul><li>Normalized scope and remaining uncertainty.</li><li>Allowance realism and selection responsibilities.</li><li>Relevant contractor experience and named team.</li><li>Management, communication, schedule, and closeout processes.</li><li>Contract and payment questions requiring professional review.</li><li>How the construction proposal fits the complete project budget.</li></ul><p>The lowest proposal is not automatically the best value. The highest is not automatically the most complete. Value is a homeowner judgment made after facts and open questions are organized.</p></section>

<section id="limits"><h2>What Bid Comparison Cannot Tell You</h2><p>A proposal comparison cannot:</p><ul><li>Select or endorse a contractor.</li><li>Guarantee quality, schedule, price, communication, or performance.</li><li>Determine whether plans or construction methods are technically adequate.</li><li>Discover every concealed condition.</li><li>Interpret contract rights, insurance coverage, lien exposure, or legal compliance.</li><li>Replace references, license and coverage verification, interviews, or professional advice.</li></ul><p>Foundation's <a href="/services/#bid-review">Bid Review™</a> organizes scope, allowances, exclusions, schedule assumptions, and contractor follow-up questions. Foundation does not maintain a preferred-provider list, accept referral compensation, select the winning bidder, or guarantee performance.</p></section>

<section id="faq"><h2>Common Questions About Comparing Contractor Bids</h2><h3>How many bids should I compare?</h3><p>There is no universal number. Compare enough qualified, responsive proposals to understand the options and make an informed decision, considering the project's size, definition, market, and procurement process. Comparable information matters more than reaching a preset count.</p><h3>What if one bid is much lower?</h3><p>Do not assume it is a bargain or a mistake. Compare scope, quantities, products, allowances, exclusions, management services, schedule, and risk assumptions, then ask the contractor to clarify material differences in writing.</p><h3>Should I make every allowance identical?</h3><p>A common comparison allowance can help expose differences, but also compare what each original allowance covers and how the agreement adjusts it. Labor, tax, delivery, markup, procurement, and related work may remain different.</p><h3>Are permits always included in a contractor bid?</h3><p>No. The proposal and written agreement should make responsibility and included fees clear. California CSLB guidance says covered home-improvement contracts need written direction about who obtains necessary permits. Confirm project requirements with the governing agency.</p><h3>Can Foundation tell me which contractor to hire?</h3><p>No. Foundation can provide an organized comparison and follow-up questions. It does not select contractors, endorse providers, maintain a preferred-provider list, or guarantee performance.</p></section>
