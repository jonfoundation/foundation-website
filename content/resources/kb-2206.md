<section id="bid-answer"><h2>How Do You Compare Contractor Bids?</h2><p>Start by comparing scope, not price. Two contractor bids can show very different totals even when both contractors are competent and honest, because the proposals may not include the same work, assumptions, allowances, materials, schedule expectations, or level of project management.</p><p>Before deciding that one contractor is expensive or another is a bargain, make sure they are actually pricing the same renovation. A useful bid comparison asks three questions: What is included? What is excluded or still uncertain? What will the homeowner likely pay once those differences are accounted for?</p><p>The goal is not to force every proposal into the same format. It is to understand the differences well enough to make an informed decision.</p></section>

<section id="same-scope"><h2>1. Confirm That Everyone Is Bidding the Same Scope</h2><p>The most important part of comparing contractor estimates is scope alignment. If one proposal includes work that another does not, the totals are not directly comparable.</p><ul><li>Demolition, protection, hauling, and disposal.</li><li>Framing, structural work, and required repairs.</li><li>Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and other trade work.</li><li>Drywall, waterproofing, tile, flooring, cabinetry, countertops, and painting.</li><li>Fixtures, appliances, finish materials, and installation.</li><li>Permits, inspections, engineering, or other professional services when applicable.</li><li>Site work, access, temporary facilities, cleanup, and project closeout.</li></ul><p>A missing scope item can make a bid look artificially low. It does not necessarily mean the contractor intentionally omitted it; the contractor may have interpreted the documents differently or assumed another party would provide the work.</p></section>

<section id="allowances"><h2>2. Compare Allowances Carefully</h2><p>Allowances are placeholders for items that have not been fully selected or priced. They are common in remodeling, but they can make two bids appear closer—or farther apart—than they really are.</p><p>For example, one contractor may carry a modest allowance for tile while another assumes a higher-end selection. The second proposal may look more expensive even though the construction labor and markup are similar.</p><ul><li>What exactly does the allowance cover: material only, labor only, or both?</li><li>What quantity or quality level was assumed?</li><li>Does the allowance reasonably match the products you are considering?</li><li>What happens if the final selection costs more or less?</li><li>Are contractor markup, tax, freight, or installation added after the allowance?</li></ul><p>The more selections you can define before final pricing, the easier it becomes to compare contractor bids accurately.</p></section>

<section id="exclusions"><h2>3. Identify Exclusions and Owner-Supplied Items</h2><p>Exclusions are just as important as inclusions. A proposal can have a lower total because certain work is explicitly excluded or expected to be handled by the homeowner.</p><p>Look for language involving painting, appliances, light fixtures, plumbing fixtures, landscaping, utility upgrades, hazardous-material testing or abatement, permit fees, engineering, temporary storage, moving, and final cleaning. The specific list will vary by project.</p><p>Do not assume an item is included simply because it appears on the drawings. If an important item is unclear, ask the contractor to confirm it in writing before making your comparison.</p></section>

<section id="normalize"><h2>4. Normalize the Bids Before Comparing Totals</h2><p>Once you understand the scope, allowances, and exclusions, create a simple comparison worksheet. Put the major scope categories in rows and the contractors in columns. Note whether each item is included, excluded, carried as an allowance, or unclear.</p><p>Then account for obvious differences. If Contractor A excludes an item that Contractor B includes, do not compare the two bottom-line prices without accounting for that missing work.</p><p>This is the step many homeowners skip. A true contractor bid comparison is less about lining up three total prices and more about normalizing the proposals so the totals mean roughly the same thing.</p></section>

<section id="price-detail"><h2>5. Look at How the Contractor Built the Price</h2><p>The amount of detail in a proposal can tell you something about how clearly the project has been defined. A higher level of detail does not automatically make a contractor better, but vague pricing creates more unanswered questions.</p><ul><li>Whether major trades and scope categories are identifiable.</li><li>Whether allowances and alternates are clearly labeled.</li><li>Whether exclusions and assumptions are easy to find.</li><li>Whether the payment structure is understandable.</li><li>Whether change-order procedures are described.</li><li>Whether the proposal matches the plans and conversations you have had.</li></ul><p>If you cannot tell what you are buying, ask for clarification before signing rather than relying on verbal assumptions. California homeowners can also review the <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Hire_A_Contractor/Home_Improvement_Contracts/What_Is_A_Contract.aspx">Contractors State License Board's current home-improvement contract guidance</a>.</p></section>

<section id="more-than-cost"><h2>6. Compare More Than Construction Cost</h2><p>Price matters, but a remodeling contractor is also providing a management service. Two contractors can approach the same project very differently.</p><p>Consider who will manage the project, how frequently the site will be supervised, how communication is handled, how schedules are developed and updated, how selections and changes are documented, and what warranty or closeout support is provided.</p><p>These services have real value even when they do not appear as separate line items. The lowest bid can still be the right choice, but it should win because it represents the <a href="/resources/how-to-choose-a-renovation-contractor">best overall fit and value</a>—not simply because the number at the bottom is smallest.</p></section>

<section id="differences"><h2>Why Are Contractor Bids So Different?</h2><p>Large differences between remodeling estimates are common. The difference can come from scope, material assumptions, labor rates, subcontractor pricing, overhead, insurance, project-management staffing, schedule, risk, markup, or simply how each contractor interpreted the project.</p><p>A large spread does not automatically prove that the high bidder is overcharging or that the low bidder missed something. Instead, use the spread as a signal that the proposals need more investigation.</p></section>

<section id="high-low"><h2>Should You Automatically Eliminate the Highest or Lowest Bid?</h2><p>No. Either one may ultimately be the right choice.</p><p>A low bid deserves additional review when it contains substantially less detail, unusually low allowances, important exclusions, or scope that does not match the other proposals. A high bid deserves the same scrutiny: determine whether it includes additional work, higher specifications, more management, a different construction approach, or simply higher pricing.</p><p>The objective is not to make all bids equal. It is to understand why they are different.</p></section>

<section id="questions"><h2>Questions to Ask Each Contractor Before You Decide</h2><ul><li>Can you confirm the exact scope included in this proposal?</li><li>What are the most important exclusions or assumptions I should understand?</li><li>Which items are allowances, and how were those allowance amounts established?</li><li>Are permit fees, inspections, engineering, and debris removal included where applicable?</li><li>Are there items shown on the plans that are not included in your price?</li><li>How are change orders priced and approved?</li><li>What could reasonably cause the contract amount to increase?</li><li>What schedule are you anticipating, and what assumptions does that schedule depend on?</li><li>Who will manage my project day to day?</li><li>What warranty and closeout process are included?</li></ul></section>

<section id="mistakes"><h2>Common Bid-Comparison Mistakes</h2><ul><li>Comparing only the total price instead of the <a href="/resources/how-to-create-a-renovation-budget">underlying scope</a>.</li><li>Assuming the drawings guarantee that every contractor included every item.</li><li>Ignoring allowances because they are already included in the total.</li><li>Failing to account for owner-supplied or excluded work.</li><li>Treating every price difference as contractor markup.</li><li>Choosing a contractor before resolving major unanswered questions.</li><li>Focusing so heavily on price that communication, management, schedule, and fit are ignored.</li></ul></section>

<section id="independent-help"><h2>When an Independent Bid Review Can Help</h2><p>Contractor proposals are often written in different formats, which can make a meaningful comparison difficult for someone who does not review construction pricing regularly.</p><p>An independent <a href="/resources/what-is-a-renovation-coach">renovation coach or consultant</a> can help organize the proposals, identify scope gaps, compare allowances and exclusions, and develop questions for the contractors. The purpose is not to select the contractor for you or negotiate behind your back. It is to help you understand what each proposal actually represents before you make the decision.</p><p>This type of review can be handled virtually in many cases because the analysis is based primarily on plans, scopes, proposals, estimates, and supporting project documents.</p></section>

<div class="article-callout"><h3>Foundation Tip</h3><p>If two bids differ significantly, do not start by asking, “Which contractor is too expensive?” Start by asking, “What is different between these two proposals?” Price differences make much more sense once the scope, allowances, exclusions, and assumptions are visible.</p></div>
