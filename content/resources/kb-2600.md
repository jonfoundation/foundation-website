<p>A home addition can create the space a household needs without giving up the home, neighborhood or property it already values. It can also be one of the most complex types of residential renovation because new construction must connect to an existing building, existing utilities and an occupied property.</p>
<p>This hub organizes Foundation’s home addition guidance from the earliest decision through final closeout. Use it to understand the process, identify the right questions and move into professional design and construction with clearer expectations.</p>
<section id="start-with-the-problem-not-the-square-footage"><h2>Start With the Problem, Not the Square Footage</h2>
<p>Homeowners often begin with a size—such as a 400-square-foot addition—or a room list. A stronger starting point is the problem the project needs to solve.</p>
<p>Document who needs more space, what is not working now, which rooms must relate to each other, how long the home should support the household and whether the location remains the right long-term fit. The same goal might be addressed through an addition, interior reconfiguration, ADU, conversion or move.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-first-home-addition-questions"><h2>The First Home Addition Questions</h2>
<p>Before commissioning full design, homeowners should begin answering:</p>
<ul>
<li>What household need is driving the project?</li>
<li>Why is an addition preferable to reorganizing the existing home or moving?</li>
<li>What appears possible under the property’s zoning and site constraints?</li>
<li>How will the addition connect to the existing structure and systems?</li>
<li>What complete-project budget is realistically available?</li>
<li>Which professional team and delivery approach fit the project?</li>
<li>Can the household remain in the home during construction?</li>
<li>Which unknowns should be investigated before major design spending?</li>
</ul>
<p>The answers will change as the project develops. Early planning should make those changes more informed, not pretend to eliminate uncertainty.</p>
</section>
<section id="phase-1-decide-whether-an-addition-is-the-right-move"><h2>Phase 1: Decide Whether an Addition Is the Right Move</h2>
<p>An addition should support the household’s long-term goals and make sense for the specific property. Evaluate lifestyle, location, budget, disruption, future flexibility and alternative ways to create space.</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/resources/is-a-home-addition-right-for-you">Is a Home Addition Right for You?</a></li>
<li><a href="/resources/types-of-home-additions">Types of Home Additions: Which One Fits Your Goals?</a></li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="phase-2-define-the-scope"><h2>Phase 2: Define the Scope</h2>
<p>A useful scope describes the outcomes the household needs, not only the rooms to be added. It should address adjacencies, circulation, storage, accessibility, building systems, finishes and how the existing home will change.</p>
<p>A larger addition does not automatically create a better result. The new space and existing floor plan must work together.</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/resources/how-to-define-home-addition-scope">How to Define the Scope of a Home Addition</a></li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="phase-3-test-property-specific-feasibility"><h2>Phase 3: Test Property-Specific Feasibility</h2>
<p>Before full design, investigate the property conditions most likely to affect the concept. Preliminary due diligence may review available records, jurisdiction, zoning, site relationships, existing structure, utilities, likely approvals and major unknowns.</p>
<p>Feasibility is rarely a simple yes or no. A responsible review identifies what appears possible, which assumptions are being made and what must be confirmed by surveys, architects, engineers, utilities or public agencies.</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/resources/home-addition-feasibility-study">What Is a Home Addition Feasibility Study?</a></li>
</ul>
</section>

It provides independent preliminary pre-design due diligence that organizes available property information, likely constraints, open questions and recommended next steps.</p>

</section>
<section id="phase-4-understand-structural-and-building-connections"><h2>Phase 4: Understand Structural and Building Connections</h2>
<p>A home addition does not stand alone. Its foundation, framing, roof, waterproofing, floor elevations and finishes must connect to an existing building that may have been constructed under different standards or modified over time.</p>
<p>Early structural and existing-condition investigation can reveal which concepts deserve further development and which assumptions carry significant risk.</p>
</section>
<section id="phase-5-establish-a-complete-budget"><h2>Phase 5: Establish a Complete Budget</h2>
<p>Home addition budgets must include more than the visible construction. Design, engineering, surveys, permits, utility work, demolition, temporary protection, site access, escalation, owner selections and contingency can all affect the amount required.</p>
<p>Early pricing should be treated as planning information until the scope, documents, site conditions and contractor assumptions are sufficiently developed.</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/resources/how-much-does-a-home-addition-cost">How Much Does a Home Addition Cost?</a></li>
<li><a href="/resources/how-to-build-home-addition-budget">How to Build a Realistic Home Addition Budget</a></li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="phase-6-build-the-right-project-team"><h2>Phase 6: Build the Right Project Team</h2>
<p>The project may involve an architect or residential designer, structural engineer, civil or geotechnical consultants, surveyor, energy consultant, contractor, specialty trades and public or utility agencies. The exact team depends on the scope and property.</p>
<p>Select the delivery approach and define responsibilities before assuming one company includes every required service.</p>
</section>
<section id="phase-7-coordinate-building-systems-and-approvals"><h2>Phase 7: Coordinate Building Systems and Approvals</h2>
<p>New space can increase demands on plumbing, sewer, electrical service, heating, cooling, ventilation and the building envelope. Capacity and routing should be evaluated alongside the floor plan rather than deferred until construction.</p>
<p>Permitting may involve planning, building, public works, utilities or specialized reviews. Requirements vary by property and jurisdiction.</p>
</section>
<section id="phase-8-prepare-for-construction"><h2>Phase 8: Prepare for Construction</h2>
<p>Addition construction affects the house, yard and household. Access routes, temporary barriers, utilities, pets, belongings, work hours, parking and communication should be planned before mobilization.</p>
<p>Early procurement planning is also important. Windows, doors, equipment, cabinetry and specialty materials may require decisions well before installation.</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/resources/how-long-does-a-home-addition-take">How Long Does a Home Addition Take?</a></li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="phase-9-live-through-the-work-safely"><h2>Phase 9: Live Through the Work Safely</h2>
<p>Remaining at home during construction may be possible, but it should not be assumed. The decision depends on separation, access, utilities, project phase, household needs and contractor safety requirements.</p>
<p>Dust, noise, security and changing circulation affect more than comfort. They require a coordinated site plan and clear expectations.</p>
</section>
<section id="phase-10-manage-changes-and-quality"><h2>Phase 10: Manage Changes and Quality</h2>
<p>Existing conditions, design development, owner decisions and agency requirements can create changes. A disciplined process documents the cause, scope, price and schedule effect before work proceeds whenever practical.</p>
<p>Quality control should focus on planned milestones, approved documents and professional responsibilities rather than homeowner micromanagement.</p>
</section>
<section id="phase-11-complete-inspections-and-closeout"><h2>Phase 11: Complete Inspections and Closeout</h2>
<p>The project is not complete when the major construction appears finished. Final inspections, correction work, punch-list items, operating information, warranties, permits and occupancy documentation still need to be resolved.</p>
</section>
<section id="review-the-most-common-planning-mistakes"><h2>Review the Most Common Planning Mistakes</h2>
<p>Many addition problems begin before construction: goals that remain vague, property constraints investigated too late, incomplete budgets, unclear professional scopes or decisions made before their downstream effects are understood.</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/resources/common-home-addition-planning-mistakes">Common Home Addition Planning Mistakes to Avoid</a></li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="a-practical-home-addition-planning-sequence"><h2>A Practical Home Addition Planning Sequence</h2>
<ul>
<li>Define the household problem and long-term goals.</li>
<li>Compare an addition with renovation, ADU and moving alternatives.</li>
<li>Establish a preliminary scope and realistic budget boundary.</li>
<li>Investigate property records, zoning, site, structure and utilities.</li>
<li>Identify the professionals and delivery approach likely to be needed.</li>
<li>Develop the design and update the budget as information improves.</li>
<li>Complete engineering, approvals and construction documents.</li>
<li>Align proposals, contracts, schedule and owner responsibilities.</li>
<li>Prepare the property and household before construction.</li>
<li>Manage changes, quality, inspections and closeout through documented processes.</li>
</ul>
</section>
