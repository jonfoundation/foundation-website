<p>A property may appear to have enough space for an accessory dwelling unit, but available yard area alone does not establish that the proposed location is buildable.</p>
<p>Zoning, setbacks, height limits, lot coverage and other development standards help determine where an ADU may be placed, how large it may be and how it relates to the primary home and neighboring properties.</p>
<p>These rules vary by jurisdiction, property and ADU configuration. They also interact with fire safety, building-code requirements, easements, utilities, drainage and other site conditions.</p>
<p>This guide explains the major development standards homeowners should investigate before committing to an ADU design.</p>
<section id="start-by-confirming-the-governing-jurisdiction"><h2>Start by Confirming the Governing Jurisdiction</h2>
<p>A mailing address does not always identify the agency that regulates a property. A property may be located within an incorporated city, an unincorporated county area or another special planning jurisdiction.</p>
<p>Before relying on an online zoning summary, confirm:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>The city or county responsible for planning and building permits</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The property’s zoning designation</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether the property is governed by a planned district or specific plan</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether residential use is permitted</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether the property contains a single-family or multifamily residential use</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Which utility, fire and special districts serve the property</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether state-level ADU protections apply</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether any additional agencies have approval authority</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Using the wrong jurisdiction’s requirements can lead to an incorrect conclusion about eligibility, setbacks, height or the number of units permitted.</p>
</section>
<section id="what-does-zoning-control"><h2>What Does Zoning Control?</h2>
<p>Zoning establishes the types of uses and development generally allowed on a property. For an ADU, zoning research may help determine:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Whether an ADU is permitted with the existing or proposed residential use</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Which ADU configurations may be available</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether the lot is treated as single-family or multifamily</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>How many ADUs or JADUs may be permitted</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether objective design standards apply</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The base-zone setbacks and height limits</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether lot coverage or floor-area restrictions apply</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether parking is required</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether special overlays modify the standard development rules</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Zoning eligibility is an important first step, but it does not confirm that a particular ADU design will work. The proposal must also be evaluated against the physical and regulatory conditions of the property.</p>
</section>
<section id="understand-the-difference-between-eligibility-and-feasibility"><h2>Understand the Difference Between Eligibility and Feasibility</h2>
<p>Eligibility asks whether the applicable regulations provide a path for creating an ADU.</p>
<p>Feasibility asks whether a specific ADU can be designed, permitted and constructed on the property in a way that supports the homeowner’s goals and budget.</p>
<p>A property may be eligible for an ADU but still face significant constraints involving:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Limited buildable area</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Difficult construction access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Utility capacity or connection routes</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Steep slopes or retaining conditions</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Drainage requirements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Easements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fire access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Existing structures</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Coastal or historic review</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Protected trees or environmental conditions</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Privacy and outdoor-space conflicts</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not treat confirmation that “ADUs are allowed” as approval of a particular size, location or configuration.</p>
</section>
<section id="what-is-a-setback"><h2>What Is a Setback?</h2>
<p>A setback is the required distance between a structure and a property line, street, another structure or other defined site feature.</p>
<p>Common setback categories include:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Front-yard setback</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Interior side-yard setback</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Street side-yard setback on a corner lot</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Rear-yard setback</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Separation between structures</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Setback from an easement</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Setback from a slope, environmentally sensitive area or other protected feature</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fire- or brush-management separation</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Different portions of an ADU may be subject to different requirements. Exterior stairs, decks, balconies, roof overhangs, equipment, retaining walls and other improvements may not receive the same setback treatment as the enclosed living area.</p>
<p>A site plan should identify each applicable setback rather than showing only a general distance from the ADU walls to the property lines.</p>
</section>
<section id="side-and-rear-setbacks-may-receive-special-adu-treatment"><h2>Side and Rear Setbacks May Receive Special ADU Treatment</h2>
<p>Many jurisdictions allow qualifying ADUs to use reduced side and rear setbacks compared with the primary residence. In California, state law provides important protections for certain ADUs using four-foot side and rear setbacks.</p>
<p>That does not mean every ADU must be placed four feet from the property line—or that four feet resolves every code issue.</p>
<p>The proposed location may still be affected by:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Fire-separation requirements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Limits on wall openings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Roof and eave projections</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Drainage at the property line</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Foundation excavation</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Existing fences and retaining walls</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Utility easements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Access for construction and maintenance</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Neighboring structures</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fire-department access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Local standards for taller ADUs</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A setback permitted by zoning may still create an inefficient, expensive or difficult-to-maintain building relationship.</p>
</section>
<section id="front-yard-rules-are-often-different"><h2>Front-Yard Rules Are Often Different</h2>
<p>Front-yard requirements are frequently more restrictive than side- and rear-yard rules.</p>
<p>Some ADU laws provide a path for a qualifying unit even when other development standards would otherwise prevent construction. However, a homeowner should not assume that the front yard is automatically available because the backyard is constrained.</p>
<p>Front-yard feasibility may depend on:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>The base-zone setback</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Visibility and sight-distance requirements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Driveway and parking access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Utility locations</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Street trees</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Public easements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Grading and drainage</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Neighborhood or historic standards</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>State-level protections</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether another feasible location exists</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A front-yard ADU can also change the relationship between the home, street, entrances and outdoor space. Regulatory permission is only part of the design decision.</p>
</section>
<section id="setbacks-are-measured-from-legal-property-lines"><h2>Setbacks Are Measured From Legal Property Lines</h2>
<p>Fences, hedges, walls and the edge of a driveway do not necessarily identify the legal property boundary.</p>
<p>Setback analysis should be based on reliable property information. Depending on the lot and project, that may require:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Recorded maps</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Title information</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Easement documents</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>A boundary survey</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>A topographic survey</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Existing permit records</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Verification of public rights-of-way</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Designing from an assumed fence line can create major problems if later survey work identifies a different boundary.</p>
</section>
<section id="easements-can-reduce-the-buildable-area"><h2>Easements Can Reduce the Buildable Area</h2>
<p>An easement gives another party certain rights to use or access part of a property. Common examples include easements for:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Sewer</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Water</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Storm drainage</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Electrical service</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Gas service</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Communications</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Public access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Private access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Shared driveways</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Slopes or drainage facilities</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>An easement does not always prohibit every improvement within its limits, but it can restrict permanent construction or require approval from the easement holder.</p>
<p>Easements should be identified before the homeowner chooses an ADU location. Discovering one after design begins may require relocation, redesign or additional agency and utility coordination.</p>
</section>
<section id="height-is-more-than-a-single-measurement"><h2>Height Is More Than a Single Measurement</h2>
<p>An ADU height limit may appear straightforward, but the measurement method matters.</p>
<p>The applicable rules may define height based on:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Existing or finished grade</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The highest point of the roof</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Roof type or pitch</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Average grade</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>A sloping site</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The relationship to the primary dwelling</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The number of stories</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Proximity to transit</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether the property contains a multifamily building</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Whether the ADU is attached or detached</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The maximum height permitted by zoning is not necessarily the maximum practical building height. Structural floor systems, ceiling heights, roof drainage, energy requirements and site grading all consume part of the available dimension.</p>
<p>A design that technically fits within the height limit may still need to address privacy, shadowing, windows, exterior stairs and its relationship to neighboring properties.</p>
</section>
<section id="one-story-and-two-story-adus-present-different-tradeoffs"><h2>One-Story and Two-Story ADUs Present Different Tradeoffs</h2>
<p>A two-story ADU may preserve more yard area, but it can introduce additional complexity.</p>
<p>Potential considerations include:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Taller foundation and structural demands</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Interior or exterior stairs</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Reduced usable floor area due to circulation</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Privacy impacts</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Window placement</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fire access and separation</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Increased construction access requirements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>More complex exterior work</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Height-related setbacks</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Neighborhood or historic standards</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Accessibility limitations</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A one-story ADU may be easier to use and more compatible with aging-in-place goals, but it occupies more of the site.</p>
<p>The decision should respond to the intended use, lot conditions, available footprint and complete project cost—not only the maximum height allowed.</p>
</section>
<section id="what-is-lot-coverage"><h2>What Is Lot Coverage?</h2>
<p>Lot coverage generally measures the portion of a property occupied by buildings or other defined improvements.</p>
<p>The exact calculation varies. A jurisdiction may include or exclude features such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>The primary residence</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The proposed ADU</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Garages and accessory structures</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Covered patios</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Roof overhangs</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Decks</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Balconies</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Driveways and other paving</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Pools</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Retaining structures</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Lot coverage is not always the same as floor-area ratio. A two-story building may have more total floor area while maintaining a smaller ground-level footprint.</p>
<p>Homeowners should verify the local definition, calculation method and any ADU-specific exceptions before using a coverage percentage.</p>
</section>
<section id="floor-area-ratio-and-open-space-rules-may-also-apply"><h2>Floor-Area Ratio and Open-Space Rules May Also Apply</h2>
<p>Floor-area ratio, commonly called FAR, compares the total regulated building area with the lot area. A jurisdiction may also require a minimum amount of open space or landscaped area.</p>
<p>These standards can affect the ADU even when the proposed footprint appears to fit within the setbacks.</p>
<p>Important questions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Which existing areas count toward FAR?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Are garages included?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Does the second story count fully?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Are covered outdoor areas included?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Does the ADU receive a statutory or local exemption?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Is required open space measured for the entire property or each unit?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Can multiple development standards be applied in a way that prevents a protected ADU?</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The design team should document the calculations rather than relying on a visual estimate of how much yard remains.</p>
</section>
<section id="california-protects-certain-adus-from-restrictive-standards"><h2>California Protects Certain ADUs From Restrictive Standards</h2>
<p>California law limits the use of lot coverage, floor-area ratio, open-space, front-setback and minimum-lot-size standards when those rules would prevent certain qualifying ADUs.</p>
<p>Current state guidance protects a path for an ADU of at least 800 square feet with four-foot side and rear setbacks when the project complies with the other applicable requirements.</p>
<p>California also establishes minimum height allowances for qualifying attached and detached ADUs. The protected height can vary based on the ADU configuration, proximity to qualifying transit and whether the property contains a multistory multifamily building.</p>
<p>These protections are explained more fully in KB-2711, California ADU Laws: What Homeowners Need to Know.</p>
<p>They should not be interpreted as a universal property plan. Easements, fire safety, building-code requirements, drainage, utilities and other site conditions may still affect where and how the protected unit can be constructed.</p>
</section>
<section id="building-code-requirements-still-apply-near-property-lines"><h2>Building-Code Requirements Still Apply Near Property Lines</h2>
<p>Zoning and building codes address different issues.</p>
<p>Zoning may allow an ADU to be placed close to a property line, while the building code may regulate:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Fire-resistance ratings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Exterior wall construction</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Windows and other openings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Roof projections</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Eaves</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Vent openings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Foundation work</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Emergency escape and rescue openings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Separation from neighboring buildings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Access for maintenance</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A proposed zero-foot or reduced setback does not mean a conventional wall, window and roof design can simply be moved to the property line.</p>
<p>The architect, designer and other qualified professionals should coordinate zoning placement with the construction requirements created by that placement.</p>
</section>
<section id="mapped-overlays-can-change-the-analysis"><h2>Mapped Overlays Can Change the Analysis</h2>
<p>Base zoning is only one layer of property regulation. An overlay or mapped condition may add requirements related to:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Coastal development</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Historic resources</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Very high fire hazard severity</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Brush management</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Flood hazards</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Environmentally sensitive lands</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Hillsides or steep slopes</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Airports</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Planned districts</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Transit areas</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Parking impact areas</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>An overlay does not always make an ADU infeasible. It may change the documentation, design standards, review agencies, timeline or cost.</p>
<p>The property’s mapped conditions should be identified during early feasibility—not after the floor plan is complete.</p>
</section>
<section id="existing-structures-affect-the-available-building-envelope"><h2>Existing Structures Affect the Available Building Envelope</h2>
<p>The ADU must work with the primary home and every other structure that will remain on the property.</p>
<p>Early planning should document:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Legally permitted buildings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Garages and accessory structures</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Covered patios and decks</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Retaining walls</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Pools and equipment</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Existing exterior stairs</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Required access paths</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fire separation between structures</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Windows that provide required light or ventilation</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Building projections and roof overhangs</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>An unpermitted structure may complicate the analysis, but it does not always need to be corrected merely because an ADU is proposed. The applicable agency and project professionals should determine whether it presents a safety issue or is affected by the new work.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-buildable-envelope-is-not-the-same-as-the-best-adu-location"><h2>The Buildable Envelope Is Not the Same as the Best ADU Location</h2>
<p>After zoning, setbacks, height and lot-coverage rules are mapped, the remaining area may be described as the buildable envelope.</p>
<p>That envelope identifies where some form of construction may be possible. It does not determine where the ADU should go.</p>
<p>A strong location should also consider:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Privacy for both households</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Direct and safe access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Relationship to outdoor space</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Views and natural light</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Utility connection distance</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Sewer depth and routing</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Drainage</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Construction access</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Material staging</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Long-term maintenance</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Noise</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Neighboring windows and yards</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The smallest regulatory envelope is not necessarily the least expensive option. A slightly different location may reduce trenching, retaining work, utility upgrades or construction difficulty.</p>
</section>
<section id="what-should-be-shown-on-an-early-adu-site-plan"><h2>What Should Be Shown on an Early ADU Site Plan?</h2>
<p>An early site plan should contain enough information to evaluate the property relationships before the homeowner invests in a detailed floor plan.</p>
<p>Depending on the project, it may show:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Legal property boundaries</p>
</li>
<li>

<section id="common-development-standard-mistakes"><h2>Common Development-Standard Mistakes</h2>
<h3 id="starting-with-a-generic-floor-plan">Starting with a generic floor plan</h3>
<p>A stock or preapproved plan may not fit the available building envelope, height limit, utility strategy or construction access.</p>
<h3 id="measuring-from-a-fence-instead-of-the-legal-property-line">Measuring from a fence instead of the legal property line</h3>
<p>A fence is not reliable evidence of ownership boundaries or easements.</p>
<h3 id="assuming-a-four-foot-setback-resolves-every-issue">Assuming a four-foot setback resolves every issue</h3>
<p>Zoning setbacks do not replace fire-separation, drainage, foundation, utility or maintenance requirements.</p>
<h3 id="looking-only-at-side-and-rear-setbacks">Looking only at side and rear setbacks</h3>
<p>Front-yard, street-side, structure-separation and overlay requirements may be equally important.</p>
<h3 id="using-the-maximum-height-as-the-design-target">Using the maximum height as the design target</h3>
<p>The maximum legal height may create added cost, privacy concerns or more restrictive setbacks.</p>
<h3 id="ignoring-lot-coverage-and-far">Ignoring lot coverage and FAR</h3>
<p>A proposed ADU may fit within the yard setbacks but still affect a coverage or floor-area calculation.</p>
<h3 id="finding-overlays-after-completing-the-design">Finding overlays after completing the design</h3>
<p>Coastal, historic, fire, hillside and environmental conditions can change the approval path.</p>
<h3 id="treating-eligibility-as-feasibility">Treating eligibility as feasibility</h3>
<p>The existence of a legal ADU pathway does not establish that the preferred project is practical or affordable.</p>
</section>
<section id="a-better-order-for-evaluating-development-standards"><h2>A Better Order for Evaluating Development Standards</h2>
<h3 id="confirm-jurisdiction-and-zoning">1. Confirm jurisdiction and zoning</h3>
<p>Identify the correct city or county, base zone, planned district and residential use.</p>
<h3 id="identify-state-and-local-adu-pathways">2. Identify state and local ADU pathways</h3>
<p>Determine which ADU configurations and protections may apply to the property.</p>
<h3 id="map-the-legal-property-information">3. Map the legal property information</h3>
<p>Verify boundaries, easements, rights-of-way and existing legal structures.</p>
<h3 id="identify-overlays-and-special-conditions">4. Identify overlays and special conditions</h3>
<p>Research coastal, historic, fire, flood, hillside, environmental and other mapped requirements.</p>
<h3 id="map-the-development-standards">5. Map the development standards</h3>
<p>Show front, street-side, side and rear setbacks, height limits, lot coverage, FAR and open-space requirements.</p>
<h3 id="add-technical-site-constraints">6. Add technical site constraints</h3>
<p>Consider fire separation, utilities, drainage, slopes, retaining walls and construction access.</p>
<h3 id="compare-possible-adu-locations">7. Compare possible ADU locations</h3>
<p>Evaluate attached, detached and conversion options against the homeowner’s goals and property conditions.</p>
<h3 id="confirm-unresolved-assumptions">8. Confirm unresolved assumptions</h3>
<p>Identify what requires survey, agency interpretation, engineering or other professional input.</p>
<h3 id="develop-the-floor-plan">9. Develop the floor plan</h3>
<p>Design within a reasonably verified site strategy rather than forcing a generic plan onto the property.</p>
</section>

<section id="the-rules-define-the-envelopeplanning-determines-the-project"><h2>The Rules Define the Envelope—Planning Determines the Project</h2>
<p>Zoning, setbacks, height and lot-coverage rules establish the regulatory framework for an ADU. They do not determine whether the resulting unit will function well, fit the homeowner’s budget or improve the property.</p>
<p>The right first step is to understand the property and the applicable rules before becoming attached to a particular floor plan.</p>
<p>Once the likely building envelope is understood, the homeowner and project team can make better decisions about ADU type, location, size, access, utilities, design and cost.</p>
</section>
<section id="jurisdiction-note"><h2>Jurisdiction Note</h2>
<p>ADU regulations vary significantly by state and local jurisdiction and may change over time. Requirements also depend on the property, existing structures, mapped conditions and proposed ADU configuration.</p>
<p>Homeowners should verify current requirements with the applicable planning, building, fire, utility and other agencies and obtain property-specific guidance from qualified professionals before relying on general information.</p>
</section>
