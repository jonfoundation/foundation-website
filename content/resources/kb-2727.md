<p>Accessory dwelling unit budgets rarely increase because of one dramatic decision.</p>
<p>More often, the total grows through a series of assumptions that were never verified, scope items that were omitted, property conditions discovered late and decisions made after pricing.</p>
<p>A realistic budget cannot eliminate every surprise. It can make the likely cost drivers visible early enough for the homeowner to investigate, plan and maintain an appropriate contingency.</p>
<p>This guide focuses on the conditions and scope gaps that commonly push an ADU beyond its preliminary budget.</p>
<section id="why-early-adu-numbers-change"><h2>Why Early ADU Numbers Change</h2>
<p>An early estimate is usually based on incomplete information.</p>
<p>At that stage, the homeowner may not yet have:</p>
<ul>
<li>A verified survey</li>
<li>A confirmed ADU location</li>
<li>A coordinated utility strategy</li>
<li>Structural or soils information</li>
<li>Complete permit requirements</li>
<li>Final selections</li>
<li>Construction documents</li>
<li>Comparable contractor proposals</li>
</ul>
<p>The number may be useful for initial decision-making, but it should be presented with its assumptions and limitations.</p>
<p>As the project becomes more defined, unknown conditions become real scope. A budget increase does not always mean someone made a mistake. It may mean the earlier number did not yet represent the complete project.</p>
</section>
<section id="site-access-can-change-the-cost-of-almost-everything"><h2>Site Access Can Change the Cost of Almost Everything</h2>
<p>Rear-yard ADUs are often built where access is narrow and the primary home remains occupied.</p>
<p>Limited access can affect:</p>
<ul>
<li>Excavation equipment</li>
<li>Concrete placement</li>
<li>Lumber and material deliveries</li>
<li>Crane or lifting operations</li>
<li>Debris removal</li>
<li>Worker travel</li>
<li>Temporary protection</li>
<li>Staging and storage</li>
<li>Construction duration</li>
</ul>
<p>A task that would be straightforward on an open site may require smaller equipment, more hand labor and repeated material handling.</p>
<p>Access should be evaluated before pricing. The project team should identify the route from the street to the work area, available clearances, items that must be removed and how the property will be restored.</p>
</section>
<section id="demolition-and-property-restoration-are-often-underestimated"><h2>Demolition and Property Restoration Are Often Underestimated</h2>
<p>Creating access and preparing the site may require removal of more than the building footprint.</p>
<p>Potential work includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Fences and gates</li>
<li>Patios and walkways</li>
<li>Landscaping and irrigation</li>
<li>Sheds and accessory structures</li>
<li>Trees or roots</li>
<li>Retaining walls</li>
<li>Driveway areas</li>
<li>Existing utility lines</li>
<li>Pool or mechanical equipment</li>
</ul>
<p>The budget must also address what happens afterward.</p>
<p>Replacement fencing, paving, landscaping, drainage, paint and repairs to the primary home may fall outside the advertised ADU construction price unless they are included explicitly.</p>
</section>
<section id="grading-soil-and-drainage-can-reshape-the-project"><h2>Grading, Soil and Drainage Can Reshape the Project</h2>
<p>A yard that appears flat may still contain drainage, soil or elevation conditions that affect the foundation and site work.</p>
<p>Possible cost drivers include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Undocumented fill</li>
<li>Expansive or weak soil</li>
<li>Slope</li>
<li>Retaining conditions</li>
<li>Excavation and soil export</li>
<li>Imported fill</li>
<li>Groundwater</li>
<li>Stormwater requirements</li>
<li>Drainage paths from neighboring properties</li>
<li>Protecting the primary home from redirected water</li>
</ul>
<p>The project may require a topographic survey, geotechnical input, civil engineering, retaining design or a more complex foundation.</p>
<p>Drainage should not be treated as a final landscaping decision. It affects the location, elevation and long-term performance of the ADU.</p>
</section>
<section id="sewer-routing-can-become-a-major-budget-surprise"><h2>Sewer Routing Can Become a Major Budget Surprise</h2>
<p>A preliminary plan may show a short line between the ADU and the existing sewer. That line does not confirm that gravity flow, depth, capacity and access will work.</p>
<p>Important questions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Where is the existing sewer connection?</li>
<li>What is its depth and condition?</li>
<li>Can the ADU drain by gravity?</li>
<li>Will the line cross the primary home, driveway or other improvements?</li>
<li>Are cleanouts accessible?</li>
<li>Is a pump required?</li>
<li>Does the agency require a separate connection?</li>
<li>What must be demolished and restored for trenching?</li>
</ul>
<p>A longer route may sometimes be less expensive if it avoids a building, retaining wall or difficult excavation.</p>
<p>The plumbing strategy should be investigated before the ADU location and finished-floor elevation are finalized.</p>
</section>
<section id="electrical-capacity-is-not-confirmed-by-an-empty-breaker-space"><h2>Electrical Capacity Is Not Confirmed by an Empty Breaker Space</h2>
<p>An electrical panel may appear to have room, but available breaker positions do not prove that the service can support another dwelling.</p>
<p>The project may require:</p>
<ul>
<li>Load calculations</li>
<li>A panel replacement</li>
<li>A service upgrade</li>
<li>Utility-provider review</li>
<li>Trenching</li>
<li>A new meter arrangement</li>
<li>Relocation of equipment</li>
<li>Changes to overhead or underground service</li>
<li>Coordination with solar or battery systems</li>
</ul>
<p>Utility-provider work can also affect the schedule independently of the building permit.</p>
<p>The budget should distinguish between work performed by the contractor and work charged or completed by the utility provider.</p>
</section>
<section id="water-gas-and-communications-still-require-defined-routes"><h2>Water, Gas and Communications Still Require Defined Routes</h2>
<p>Water, gas and communications may be smaller line items than the building structure, but their routing can affect excavation, paving and coordination.</p>
<p>The project team should determine:</p>
<ul>
<li>Connection points</li>
<li>Required pipe or conduit sizes</li>
<li>Metering approach</li>
<li>Existing capacity</li>
<li>Backflow or pressure requirements</li>
<li>Trench sharing and separation</li>
<li>Restoration</li>
<li>Provider responsibilities</li>
</ul>
<p>If the ADU will be all-electric, the gas scope may be reduced, but the electrical demand may increase.</p>
</section>
<section id="existing-structures-create-hidden-risk"><h2>Existing Structures Create Hidden Risk</h2>
<p>Garage conversions, interior conversions and attached ADUs rely on construction that may not have been designed, documented or maintained for the proposed use.</p>
<p>Potential discoveries include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Inadequate foundations</li>
<li>Damaged framing</li>
<li>Moisture intrusion</li>
<li>Termite or decay damage</li>
<li>Low ceiling heights</li>
<li>Unpermitted alterations</li>
<li>Missing fire separation</li>
<li>Inadequate insulation</li>
<li>Sloped or cracked slabs</li>
<li>Outdated electrical or plumbing systems</li>
</ul>
<p>The existence of walls and a roof does not guarantee that they can be reused economically.</p>
<p>An existing-condition investigation can reduce uncertainty, but some concealed conditions will remain unknown until construction opens the assembly.</p>
</section>
<section id="connecting-to-the-primary-home-adds-complexity"><h2>Connecting to the Primary Home Adds Complexity</h2>
<p>An attached ADU or addition must integrate new work with the existing house.</p>
<p>Cost can increase because of:</p>
<ul>
<li>Structural openings</li>
<li>Roof tie-ins</li>
<li>Foundation connections</li>
<li>Exterior finish matching</li>
<li>Relocation of windows or doors</li>
<li>Changes to existing rooms</li>
<li>Temporary weather protection</li>
<li>Mechanical, plumbing and electrical conflicts</li>
<li>Repair of finishes beyond the immediate connection</li>
</ul>
<p>A small connection on the plan can involve several trades and a wider area of disruption than expected.</p>
</section>
<section id="design-complexity-has-a-construction-cost"><h2>Design Complexity Has a Construction Cost</h2>
<p>Complicated shapes, rooflines, large openings and custom details may increase engineering, labor, waste and coordination.</p>
<p>Cost-sensitive design generally benefits from:</p>
<ul>
<li>Efficient building geometry</li>
<li>Clear structural load paths</li>
<li>Repeated window and door sizes</li>
<li>Stacked or grouped plumbing</li>
<li>Practical roof drainage</li>
<li>Standard material dimensions</li>
<li>Coordinated equipment locations</li>
<li>Limited transitions between materials</li>
</ul>
<p>Simple does not mean generic or unattractive. It means using complexity where it creates meaningful value.</p>
</section>
<section id="full-adus-still-need-complete-building-systems"><h2>Full ADUs Still Need Complete Building Systems</h2>
<p>A smaller full ADU does not cost proportionately less than a larger one. Every independent ADU still needs core dwelling components such as cooking and sanitation facilities, electrical distribution, plumbing, heating, cooling or ventilation, water heating, windows, exterior doors, life-safety systems, utility connections, permits and professional services.</p>
<p>JADUs are a distinct category and should not be budgeted as though they have the same scope as a full ADU. Under current California law, a JADU must contain an efficiency kitchen rather than a conventional full kitchen. The efficiency kitchen includes cooking appliances, a food-preparation counter and storage cabinets reasonably sized for the unit. A 220-volt circuit cannot be required solely for the efficiency kitchen.</p>
<p>A California JADU may have its own sanitation facilities or share them with the primary residence. If it shares sanitation facilities, the layout must maintain the required interior access to the main living area. These choices affect plumbing, electrical work, construction scope and operating arrangements.</p>
<p>Requirements outside California vary. Confirm whether the proposed unit is an ADU, JADU or another locally defined housing type before assuming which systems and facilities are required.</p>
<p>Even when a smaller unit has a reduced scope, many design, approval, utility and construction costs remain fixed or semi-fixed. That is why a generic cost-per-square-foot number can still be misleading.</p>
</section>
<section id="permit-corrections-can-affect-more-than-the-schedule"><h2>Permit Corrections Can Affect More Than the Schedule</h2>
<p>Plan-check comments may require clarification, but some comments also change the design or scope.</p>
<p>Potential impacts include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Additional engineering</li>
<li>Revised structural systems</li>
<li>Fire-rated construction</li>
<li>Changes to openings</li>
<li>Energy upgrades</li>
<li>Added drainage information</li>
<li>Utility revisions</li>
<li>Accessibility or egress changes</li>
<li>New technical reports</li>
<li>Consultant coordination</li>
</ul>
<p>The design agreement should explain who responds to comments and whether redesign or additional consultant work is included.</p>
<p>The budget and schedule should be updated when a correction changes the project rather than treating every comment as administrative.</p>
</section>
<section id="special-property-conditions-add-review-layers"><h2>Special Property Conditions Add Review Layers</h2>
<p>Coastal, historic, fire, flood, hillside and environmental conditions can affect the project even when an ADU is generally permitted.</p>
<p>These conditions may create:</p>
<ul>
<li>Additional agency review</li>
<li>Technical reports</li>
<li>Design limitations</li>
<li>Fire-resistive construction</li>
<li>Access requirements</li>
<li>Grading or drainage work</li>
<li>Longer approval timelines</li>
<li>Additional professional coordination</li>
</ul>
<p>Mapped conditions should be researched during feasibility. Discovering them after design can create avoidable rework.</p>
</section>
<section id="allowances-can-hide-future-increases"><h2>Allowances Can Hide Future Increases</h2>
<p>An allowance is a placeholder for work or a product that has not been fully selected or defined.</p>
<p>A low allowance can make a proposal appear competitive while shifting the difference into later changes.</p>
<p>For each allowance, confirm:</p>
<ul>
<li>What it covers</li>
<li>The assumed quantity and quality</li>
<li>Whether labor is included</li>
<li>Whether tax, delivery and waste are included</li>
<li>Which accessories are required</li>
<li>When the selection must be made</li>
<li>How increases and credits are calculated</li>
</ul>
<p>Allowances should be realistic enough to support the intended project, not merely low enough to reach a desired contract total.</p>
</section>
<section id="exclusions-do-not-remove-costs-from-the-project"><h2>Exclusions Do Not Remove Costs From the Project</h2>
<p>A proposal may exclude necessary work because another party is expected to provide it or because the scope is unresolved.</p>
<p>Common exclusions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Design and engineering</li>
<li>Permit and agency fees</li>
<li>Utility-provider charges</li>
<li>Surveying</li>
<li>Testing and inspections</li>
<li>Landscaping</li>
<li>Fencing and paving</li>
<li>Appliances</li>
<li>Window coverings</li>
<li>Solar or service upgrades</li>
<li>Hazardous-material work</li>
<li>Unforeseen conditions</li>
</ul>
<p>Every exclusion should be assigned to a responsible party and carried in the master budget. An excluded item does not become optional merely because it is outside the construction contract.</p>
</section>
<section id="owner-purchased-items-can-create-secondary-costs"><h2>Owner-Purchased Items Can Create Secondary Costs</h2>
<p>A homeowner may purchase appliances, fixtures, lighting or finishes directly.</p>
<p>The budget should include more than the purchase price:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sales tax</li>
<li>Delivery</li>
<li>Storage</li>
<li>Damage or return risk</li>
<li>Installation</li>
<li>Required accessories</li>
<li>Blocking or rough-in changes</li>
<li>Coordination</li>
<li>Schedule impacts</li>
<li>Warranty responsibility</li>
</ul>
<p>A lower online price may not create a project savings if the item arrives late, is incompatible with the plans or requires additional labor.</p>
</section>
<section id="late-decisions-are-expensive"><h2>Late Decisions Are Expensive</h2>
<p>A decision made after pricing or construction begins can affect completed work and multiple trades.</p>
<p>Late changes may require:</p>
<ul>
<li>Redesign</li>
<li>Permit revisions</li>
<li>New engineering</li>
<li>Demolition</li>
<li>Reordering</li>
<li>Restocking fees</li>
<li>Expedited shipping</li>
<li>Trade remobilization</li>
<li>Schedule extensions</li>
</ul>
<p>The project team should maintain a decision schedule showing what must be selected, approved or confirmed before each phase.</p>
<p>Changes should be evaluated for both direct cost and downstream effects.</p>
</section>
<section id="long-lead-times-can-create-budget-pressure"><h2>Long Lead Times Can Create Budget Pressure</h2>
<p>Windows, electrical equipment, appliances, mechanical systems and specialty finishes may have extended procurement periods.</p>
<p>A late order can lead to:</p>
<ul>
<li>Schedule delay</li>
<li>Temporary protection</li>
<li>Substitutions</li>
<li>Expedited freight</li>
<li>Additional mobilization</li>
<li>Extended supervision and temporary facilities</li>
</ul>
<p>Confirm critical products early and understand who is responsible for procurement, submittal review and delivery tracking.</p>
</section>
<section id="price-differences-often-reflect-scope-differences"><h2>Price Differences Often Reflect Scope Differences</h2>
<p>Two contractor proposals with different totals may not represent the same project.</p>
<p>One proposal may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>More site work</li>
<li>More complete utilities</li>
<li>Higher allowances</li>
<li>Permit coordination</li>
<li>Better-defined restoration</li>
<li>Temporary protection</li>
<li>Testing and closeout</li>
</ul>
<p>Another may leave those items to the homeowner or address them through later changes.</p>
<p>Compare proposals by mapping each one into the same master budget categories. The guide to <a href="/resources/how-to-build-an-adu-budget">building an ADU budget</a> explains how to maintain that all-in owner view. Do not assume the lowest total is the lowest final cost.</p>
</section>
<section id="change-orders-are-not-all-the-same"><h2>Change Orders Are Not All the Same</h2>
<p>A change order may result from:</p>
<ul>
<li>A homeowner-requested change</li>
<li>A concealed condition</li>
<li>An agency requirement</li>
<li>Incomplete documents</li>
<li>A coordination conflict</li>
<li>A contractor omission</li>
<li>A product substitution</li>
</ul>
<p>The contract should explain how changes are documented, priced and approved.</p>
<p>Homeowners should understand the cause of a change rather than treating every added cost as equivalent. The cause helps determine whether better investigation, documentation or communication could prevent a similar issue.</p>
</section>
<section id="contingency-should-match-the-remaining-risk"><h2>Contingency Should Match the Remaining Risk</h2>
<p>Contingency is a financial reserve for uncertainty that cannot reasonably be eliminated before construction.</p>
<p>Projects may need more contingency when they involve:</p>
<ul>
<li>Older or undocumented structures</li>
<li>Conversions</li>
<li>Limited investigation</li>
<li>Difficult utilities</li>
<li>Restricted access</li>
<li>Slope or retaining work</li>
<li>Unresolved agency requirements</li>
<li>Incomplete selections</li>
</ul>
<p>Contingency should not be used to conceal known scope or force an unrealistic budget to appear complete.</p>
<p>As risks are investigated and scope becomes defined, known work should move into the appropriate budget category.</p>
</section>
<section id="questions-to-ask-before-accepting-an-adu-budget"><h2>Questions to Ask Before Accepting an ADU Budget</h2>
<ul>
<li>Does the budget cover the complete project or only one contract?</li>
<li>Which property conditions have been verified?</li>
<li>Are utility routes and capacities confirmed?</li>
<li>What demolition and restoration are included?</li>
<li>Which figures are fixed prices, estimates or allowances?</li>
<li>What is excluded?</li>
<li>Who pays agency and utility-provider charges?</li>
<li>What owner purchases remain?</li>
<li>Which decisions could change the price?</li>
<li>What risks remain concealed until construction?</li>
<li>How was the contingency determined?</li>
<li>How will changes be documented and approved?</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="how-foundation-can-help"><h2>How Foundation Can Help</h2>
<p>Foundation Renovation Coaching helps homeowners organize ADU scope, budget assumptions, exclusions and responsibilities before committing to design or construction.</p>
<p>That may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Identifying missing budget categories</li>
<li>Organizing property and utility questions</li>
<li>Reviewing allowances and exclusions</li>
<li>Comparing proposals against a consistent scope</li>
<li>Identifying unresolved decisions</li>
<li>Preparing questions for designers and builders</li>
<li>Building a more complete project-cost framework</li>
</ul>
<p>Foundation does not provide contractor estimates, engineering, architectural services or guarantees about hidden conditions. The role is to help homeowners understand what the available numbers represent and which questions remain unresolved.</p>
</section>
<section id="budget-surprises-are-often-information-gaps"><h2>Budget Surprises Are Often Information Gaps</h2>
<p>Some ADU cost changes cannot be predicted before construction. Many others can be traced to incomplete research, unclear responsibility, unrealistic allowances or a proposal that did not represent the entire project.</p>
<p>The goal is not to eliminate all uncertainty.</p>
<p>It is to investigate the highest-risk conditions, make assumptions visible and maintain a budget capable of absorbing what cannot be confirmed in advance.</p>
</section>
<section id="jurisdiction-note"><h2>Jurisdiction Note</h2>
<p>ADU requirements, utility policies, permit procedures and construction conditions vary by location and property. Confirm current requirements and property-specific conditions with the applicable agencies and qualified professionals.</p>
</section>
