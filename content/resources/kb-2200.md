<span id="no-standard-team"></span>
<h2>Who Should Be on Your Renovation Team?</h2>
<p>The right renovation team is the smallest group of qualified professionals whose skills and responsibilities match the project. Depending on the scope, that may include an architect or residential designer, interior designer, general contractor, design-build firm, engineers, specialty consultants, and an independent renovation advisor.</p>
<p>There is no universal lineup. A focused interior renovation, a structural addition, and a whole-home renovation do not require the same information or expertise. Existing conditions, the proposed work, local regulations, the homeowner’s preferences, and the amount of coordination the homeowner wants to manage all affect the right team.</p>
<p>The goal is not to assemble the largest team. It is to make sure every necessary decision has an appropriate owner and that the communication path is clear. Each professional should understand what they are responsible for, what they are not responsible for, what information they need from others, and who communicates decisions to the homeowner.</p>
</section>

<section id="start-with-project">

<h2>Start With the Project, Not a Preferred Team Structure</h2>
<p>Architect-led, designer-led, contractor-led, design-build, and independently assembled teams can each be appropriate. None is universally best. They distribute contracts, communication, design responsibility, pricing input, and coordination differently.</p>
<p>Before selecting a structure, define the problems the renovation should solve, the spaces and systems likely to be affected, known structural or site questions, the level of design exploration required, possible approvals, and how much coordination you want to manage directly.</p>
<p>Then ask each prospective professional to explain their role, agreement, deliverables, dependencies, and limits in plain language.</p>
<p>Local requirements also matter. The governing authority may be a city, county, regional agency, or another jurisdiction. Zoning, historic, coastal, environmental, wildfire, utility, energy, accessibility, or site conditions may affect the review path. Confirm the applicable requirements before assuming which professionals or documents will be needed.</p>
</section>

<section id="choose-the-guidance-that-matches-your-next-decision">

<h2>Choose the Guidance That Matches Your Next Decision</h2>
<p>If you are preparing to interview or compare contractors, start with <a href="/resources/how-to-choose-a-renovation-contractor">How Do You Choose the Right Renovation Contractor?</a> and <a href="/resources/how-to-compare-contractor-bids">How to Compare Contractor Bids for a Home Renovation</a>.</p>

<section id="professionals">

<h2>Professionals You May Encounter</h2>
<h3 id="architect">Architect</h3>
<p>An architect may help define the design, evaluate alternatives, prepare drawings and specifications, coordinate consultants, support approvals, and provide construction-phase services when those services are included in the agreement.</p>
<p>Ask which phases and deliverables are included. Licensing requirements and the types of work that require a licensed professional vary by jurisdiction, so verify the rules and credentials that apply to your project.</p>
<h3 id="residential-designer">Residential Designer</h3>
<p>A residential designer may develop layouts, exterior concepts, drawings, details, and finish direction for residential work. Services, training, permitted scope, and coordination practices vary.</p>
<p>Ask what the designer will produce, what work they are qualified or legally permitted to perform, and which consultants or licensed professionals may be required.</p>
<h3 id="interior-designer">Interior Designer</h3>
<p>An interior designer may assist with space planning, cabinetry, fixtures, finishes, lighting intent, furnishings, procurement, and the functional experience of the interior.</p>
<p>Clarify whether the scope includes technical documents, material specifications, ordering, delivery coordination, site visits, installation questions, and construction administration.</p>
<h3 id="contractor">General Contractor</h3>
<p>A general contractor typically contracts for and manages construction. Responsibilities may include field operations, trade coordination, scheduling, materials, quality-control processes, and communication about progress and changes according to the agreement.</p>
<p>Some contractors also provide preconstruction estimating or constructability input. Confirm when that involvement begins, what it includes, and whether it is a paid service.</p>
<h3 id="design-build">Design-Build Firm</h3>
<p>A design-build firm provides design and construction through one contracting or organizational structure. This can simplify certain handoffs, but the label alone does not explain who performs the design, how credentials and agreements are structured, when pricing develops, or how homeowner priorities are represented during tradeoff decisions.</p>
<p>Ask the same detailed questions you would ask separately retained designers and contractors.</p>
<h3 id="specialists">Engineers</h3>
<p>Structural, civil, geotechnical, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and other engineers may be needed depending on the proposed work, existing conditions, and agency requirements.</p>
<p>Ask who identifies the need for engineering, who retains the engineer, who coordinates the engineer’s information with the rest of the documents, and who resolves conflicts.</p>
<h3>Specialty Consultants</h3>
<p>Surveyors, energy consultants, landscape designers, accessibility specialists, hazardous-material professionals, lighting designers, and other specialists may contribute when the project or site requires their expertise.</p>
<p>The need for these services may become clear during early feasibility work, design, agency review, or investigation of existing conditions.</p>
<h3>Independent Renovation Advisor or Coach</h3>
<p>An independent renovation advisor helps homeowners organize goals, priorities, information, risks, questions, and next decisions. This role can be especially useful before the full project team is assembled or when the homeowner wants a neutral perspective on scope, sequencing, or proposals.</p>
<p>A renovation advisor does not replace architects, engineers, contractors, inspectors, attorneys, insurance professionals, or permitting authorities.</p>
</section>

<section id="coordination">

<h2>Who Coordinates the Renovation Team?</h2>
<p>Coordination can be led by different participants at different stages. A design professional may coordinate design consultants. A general contractor may coordinate construction trades. A design-build firm may coordinate design and construction within its structure. A homeowner with separate agreements may retain more coordination responsibility than expected.</p>
<p>Write down who will:</p>
<ul>
<li>Maintain the current plans, specifications, and selections</li>
<li>Check information between disciplines</li>
<li>Identify and coordinate consultants</li>
<li>Submit for permits and respond to agency comments</li>
<li>Issue pricing information and track assumptions</li>
<li>Answer design and field questions</li>
<li>Document changes and approvals</li>
<li>Communicate schedule or budget impacts</li>
<li>Keep the homeowner informed</li>
</ul>
<p>“Everyone will collaborate” is a goal, not a coordination plan.</p>
</section>

<section id="gaps-overlaps">

<h2>Where Team Structures Commonly Break Down</h2>
<p>Gaps often appear because two people assume the other person owns the same task. A homeowner may assume the contractor is coordinating design details. The contractor may expect final selections from the designer. The designer may believe the homeowner is tracking allowance and budget impacts.</p>
<p>Overlaps create a different problem. Two professionals may both believe they have authority to direct the same work, maintain separate versions of project information, or communicate conflicting instructions.</p>
<p>The solution is a responsibility map: a simple written record of who decides, who documents, who reviews, who approves, and who communicates each important item.</p>
</section>

<section id="hiring-questions">

<h2>Questions to Ask Before Hiring the Team</h2>
<ul>
<li>What outcomes, scope, and existing-condition questions are currently understood?</li>
<li>Which decisions and documents must be produced, and who will own each one?</li>
<li>What is included and expressly excluded from each agreement?</li>
<li>Which license, registration, insurance, or specialized qualification applies?</li>
<li>Who identifies and coordinates engineers or specialty consultants?</li>
<li>Who maintains the current project information and resolves conflicts?</li>
<li>Who confirms permit responsibility and communicates with the governing agency?</li>
<li>How are meetings, revisions, additional services, pricing questions, and field decisions handled?</li>
<li>What information must be complete before fees, estimates, or schedules can be relied upon?</li>
<li>How will the homeowner know when each professional’s work is complete?</li>
</ul>

<p>Once plans exist, a <a href="/services/#plan-review">Foundation Plan Review™</a> can organize questions about alignment, missing information, constructability concerns, and value opportunities. It does not replace professional design, engineering, code, or legal review.</p>
</section>

<section id="faq">

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Does every renovation need an architect?</h3>
<p>No. The answer depends on the scope, structural complexity, local rules, desired design services, and the capabilities of the other professionals involved. Confirm project-specific requirements with the governing authority and appropriately qualified professionals.</p>
<h3>Should I hire a designer or contractor first?</h3>
<p>Either sequence can work, but it changes how design, pricing, and coordination develop. Start by identifying the decisions the project needs next, then choose the professional best qualified to help make those decisions.</p>
<h3>Is design-build always easier?</h3>
<p>Design-build can reduce some contractual handoffs, but it does not eliminate the need to understand responsibilities, deliverables, pricing assumptions, or change procedures. Evaluate the actual team and agreement, not only the delivery-method label.</p>
<h3>Who is responsible for permits?</h3>
<p>Permit responsibility should be stated in the agreements. One party may prepare documents, another may submit them, and the homeowner may remain the legal applicant or responsible party. Confirm the process with the governing jurisdiction.</p>
<h3>What if I do not know which professionals I need?</h3>
<p>An early planning or feasibility step can help identify likely design, technical, approval, and construction needs before you commit to the full team.</p>
</section>

<section id="foundation-tip">

<h2>Foundation Tip</h2>
<p>Not every renovation needs every professional. The goal is to build a team whose expertise matches the project and whose responsibilities fit together without hidden gaps or conflicting assumptions.</p>
</section>

<section id="closing-thought">

<h2>Closing Thought</h2>
<p>A well-structured renovation team makes both expertise and responsibility visible. Begin with the project’s goals and complexity, verify qualifications and agreements, and resolve gaps before they become construction questions.</p>
</section>

<section id="california-resources">

<h2>California Resources</h2>
<p>Architecture licensing and permitted professional services are governed by state law. Verify credentials through the appropriate state licensing authority and discuss which services the project and jurisdiction require. For California projects, license information is available through the California Architects Board's <a href="https://www.cab.ca.gov/cons/archs/lic_search.shtml">license search</a>.</p>
<p>Verify that any required contractor license is active and appropriate for the work through the licensing authority in your state. A license check is one part of due diligence, not an endorsement or guarantee of future performance. For California projects, use CSLB's <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx">Check a License</a> service.</p>
<p>If a homeowner signs the permit as an owner-builder, responsibilities can change materially and vary by jurisdiction. California's CSLB, for example, explains that an <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Know_Risks_Of_Owner_-_Builder/The_Responsibilities_Of_An_Owner_-_Builder.aspx">owner-builder assumes responsibility</a> for the project and may take on employer and other obligations. Do not accept an owner-builder role without understanding applicable requirements and obtaining appropriate project-specific advice.</p>
<p>The written agreement should identify who obtains necessary permits. Requirements vary by state and jurisdiction; for example, California CSLB guidance says home-improvement contracts need written direction about permit responsibility. Confirm the process with the governing agency and do not sign as owner-builder without understanding the resulting obligations.</p>
</section>

<section id="professional-responsibilities-and-service-boundaries">

<h2>Professional Responsibilities and Service Boundaries</h2>
<p>This is general homeowner education, not architectural, engineering, contracting, inspection, legal, insurance, permitting, or safety advice. Confirm project-specific requirements with the governing agency and appropriately licensed or qualified professionals.</p>
<p>Review proposals and agreements together. If one party excludes a task another assumes is included, resolve it before the next commitment. Foundation can help organize and explain these distinctions, but does not select professionals, maintain a preferred-provider list, or guarantee anyone's performance.</p>
<p>Each professional owes the duties established by their role and agreement; do not assume every participant provides independent homeowner advice. Read the agreements, ask directly about responsibilities and conflicts, and obtain legal advice about contractual rights when needed.</p>
<p>Foundation does not select project professionals, maintain a preferred-provider list, accept referral compensation, or guarantee performance. Its role is to help homeowners organize information, understand choices, and prepare their own questions and decisions.</p>
