<section id="team-answer"><h2>Who Should Be on a Home Renovation Team?</h2><p><strong>The right renovation team is the smallest group of qualified professionals whose skills and responsibilities match the project. Depending on the scope, that may include an architect or residential designer, interior designer, general contractor, design-build firm, engineers, and specialty consultants. Start with the decisions and technical needs—not a preferred team structure—and confirm who will design, document, approve, price, coordinate, build, and communicate.</strong></p><p>There is no universal lineup. A focused interior renovation and a structural addition do not require the same information or expertise. The condition of the home, proposed work, governing jurisdiction, homeowner preferences, and desired level of support all shape the team.</p><p>This guide explains roles and coordination. For the full planning sequence, read <a href="/resources/how-to-plan-home-renovation">How to Plan a Home Renovation</a> and use the <a href="/resources/foundation-renovation-roadmap">Foundation Renovation Roadmap™</a> to identify your current phase. When you are ready to evaluate a builder, continue to <a href="/resources/how-to-choose-a-renovation-contractor">How to Choose the Right Renovation Contractor</a>.</p><p>This is general homeowner education, not architectural, engineering, contracting, inspection, legal, insurance, permitting, or safety advice. Confirm project-specific requirements with the governing agency and appropriately licensed or qualified professionals.</p></section>

<div class="article-callout"><h3>Role clarity matters more than team size</h3><p>Every necessary decision should have an owner. Every professional should understand what they are responsible for, what they are not responsible for, what information they need from others, and who communicates decisions to the homeowner.</p></div>

<section id="no-standard-team"><h2>There Is No Standard Renovation Team</h2><p>Team needs grow from the project. Cosmetic work may need limited design support and a properly licensed contractor. Layout changes, additions, structural work, complex building systems, historically sensitive properties, accessibility goals, or difficult sites may require more design and technical coordination.</p><p>Do not assume every professional is required, or that one company must provide every service. Also avoid removing expertise merely to simplify the org chart. The useful question is: <em>Which decisions must be made, documented, approved, and carried into construction—and who is qualified and engaged to do each one?</em></p><p>The applicable authority may be a city, county, state, or another agency, and zoning, coastal, historic, utility, wildfire, or environmental conditions may affect the review path. Requirements and professional roles vary by jurisdiction, so confirm which agencies govern the property and proposed work.</p></section>

<section id="start-with-project"><h2>Start With the Project, Not a Preferred Delivery Method</h2><p>Architect-led, designer-led, contractor-led, design-build, and independently assembled teams can each be appropriate. None is universally best. They distribute contracts, communication, design responsibility, pricing input, and coordination differently.</p><p>Before choosing a structure, describe:</p><ul><li>The problems the renovation should solve and the rooms or systems likely affected.</li><li>Known structural, site, code, utility, accessibility, or existing-condition questions.</li><li>The level of design exploration and documentation the project requires.</li><li>Which approvals may apply and who will investigate them.</li><li>When construction experience should inform design and pricing decisions.</li><li>How much coordination the homeowner wants to manage directly.</li></ul><p>Then ask each prospective professional to explain their role, agreement, deliverables, dependencies, and limits in plain language.</p></section>

<section id="architect"><h2>What Does an Architect Do?</h2><p>An architect may help define the design, evaluate alternatives, prepare drawings and specifications, coordinate engineering or other consultants, support approvals, and provide construction-phase services when those tasks are included in the agreement. The exact scope varies substantially.</p><p>Ask which project phases are included, who produces technical documents, who coordinates consultants, how contractor questions will be handled, and whether construction observation or other construction-phase involvement is included. A title alone does not define the service.</p><p>Architecture licensing and permitted professional services are governed by state law. Verify credentials through the appropriate state licensing authority and discuss which services the project and jurisdiction require. For California projects, license information is available through the California Architects Board's <a href="https://www.cab.ca.gov/cons/archs/lic_search.shtml">license search</a>.</p></section>

<section id="residential-designer"><h2>What Does a Residential Designer Do?</h2><p>A residential designer may develop layouts, exterior concepts, drawings, details, and finish direction for residential work. Services, training, permitted scope, and coordination practices vary by state and project. Some work may fall within state-specific statutory exemptions; other projects require licensed design professionals or engineers.</p><p>Ask what the designer will produce, what work they are legally and technically qualified to perform, which consultants are expected, who coordinates them, and whether the governing jurisdiction has confirmed document requirements. Do not rely on a job title as proof of licensure or project authority.</p></section>

<section id="interior-designer"><h2>What Does an Interior Designer Do?</h2><p>An interior designer may help with space planning, cabinetry, fixtures, finishes, lighting intent, furnishings, and the functional experience of the interior. Some provide extensive technical documentation and procurement; others focus on concepts and selections.</p><p>Clarify whether the scope includes measured drawings, construction details, product specifications, ordering, delivery coordination, site visits, and responses to installation questions. Determine who coordinates interior decisions with architectural documents, engineering, contractor pricing, and field conditions.</p></section>

<section id="contractor"><h2>What Does a General Contractor Do?</h2><p>A general contractor typically contracts for and manages construction: planning field operations, coordinating trades, scheduling work, obtaining materials, maintaining required records, and communicating about progress and changes according to the agreement. Preconstruction estimating or constructability input may also be offered, but it should be clearly scoped.</p><p>Verify that any required contractor license is active and appropriate for the work through the licensing authority in your state. A license check is one part of due diligence, not an endorsement or guarantee of future performance. For California projects, use CSLB's <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx">Check a License</a> service.</p><p>If a homeowner signs the permit as an owner-builder, responsibilities can change materially and vary by jurisdiction. California's CSLB, for example, explains that an <a href="https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Know_Risks_Of_Owner_-_Builder/The_Responsibilities_Of_An_Owner_-_Builder.aspx">owner-builder assumes responsibility</a> for the project and may take on employer and other obligations. Do not accept an owner-builder role without understanding applicable requirements and obtaining appropriate project-specific advice.</p></section>

<section id="design-build"><h2>What Does a Design-Build Firm Do?</h2><p>A design-build firm provides design and construction through one contracting or organizational structure. That can simplify some communication, but the name does not tell you which individuals perform design, how decisions are documented, when pricing develops, or how design and construction responsibilities are allocated.</p><p>Ask who the design professionals are, what their credentials and agreements cover, how engineering is coordinated, when the scope and price become sufficiently defined, how alternatives are evaluated, and who represents the homeowner's priorities during tradeoff decisions. Evaluate the actual people and process rather than the delivery label.</p></section>

<section id="specialists"><h2>When Might Engineers and Specialty Consultants Be Needed?</h2><p>Structural, civil, geotechnical, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, energy, landscape, accessibility, hazardous-material, surveying, or other specialists may be appropriate depending on the work and existing conditions. Their involvement may be required by an agency, recommended by a design professional, or chosen to resolve project risk.</p><p>Ask who identifies the need, who contracts with each specialist, what they will investigate or design, how their information enters the main documents, and who resolves conflicts. Safety, hazardous materials, structural adequacy, and code compliance require the relevant qualified professionals; renovation coaching is not a substitute.</p></section>

<section id="coordination"><h2>Who Coordinates the Renovation Team?</h2><p>Coordination can be led by different participants at different stages. A design professional may coordinate design consultants; a general contractor may coordinate construction trades; a design-build firm may coordinate both under its structure; or the homeowner may hold separate agreements and retain more coordination responsibility.</p><p>Write down the communication path. Identify who:</p><ul><li>Maintains the current drawings, specifications, selections, and decision records.</li><li>Checks information between disciplines and raises conflicts.</li><li>Submits for permits and responds to agency comments.</li><li>Issues pricing information and communicates scope changes.</li><li>Answers field questions and documents approved decisions.</li><li>Communicates with the homeowner, and at what intervals.</li></ul><p>“Everyone will collaborate” is a goal, not a coordination plan.</p></section>

<section id="gaps-overlaps"><h2>How Do You Identify Gaps and Overlapping Responsibilities?</h2><p>List major tasks down one side of a simple responsibility matrix and team members across the top. Mark who leads, contributes, approves, and receives information. Include investigation, design, engineering, selections, estimating, permits, procurement, construction questions, changes, inspections, and closeout.</p><p>A gap exists when no engaged party owns a necessary task. An overlap exists when two parties appear to own it but their agreements do not say who leads. Overlap can be useful when intentional; hidden overlap creates duplicated work or conflicting direction.</p><p>Review proposals and agreements together. If one party excludes a task another assumes is included, resolve it before the next commitment. Foundation can help organize and explain these distinctions, but does not select professionals, maintain a preferred-provider list, or guarantee anyone's performance.</p></section>

<section id="hiring-questions"><h2>Questions to Resolve Before Hiring Anyone</h2><ul><li>What outcomes, scope, and existing-condition questions are currently understood?</li><li>What decisions and documents will this professional be responsible for?</li><li>What is expressly excluded?</li><li>Which license, registration, insurance, or specialized qualification applies?</li><li>Who else must be engaged, and who coordinates them?</li><li>Who owns the work product and may use it if the team changes?</li><li>How are meetings, revisions, additional services, and construction questions handled?</li><li>Who confirms permit responsibilities and communicates with the agency?</li><li>What information is needed before fees, pricing, or schedules can be relied upon?</li><li>How will the homeowner know when this professional's work is complete?</li></ul><p>Once plans exist, a <a href="/services/#plan-review">Foundation Plan Review™</a> can organize questions about alignment, missing information, constructability concerns, and value opportunities. It does not replace professional design, engineering, code, or legal review.</p></section>

<section id="faq"><h2>Common Renovation Team Questions</h2><h3>Do I need both an architect and an interior designer?</h3><p>Not always. Their services may be separate, overlapping, or coordinated within one firm. The project scope and each professional's actual agreement determine whether both are useful. Compare responsibilities and deliverables rather than titles alone.</p><h3>Should I hire the contractor before or after design?</h3><p>There is no universal sequence. Early contractor input can be useful, while sufficiently developed and coordinated design information may be necessary for meaningful pricing. Choose a process that clearly defines services, preserves needed expertise, and supports informed commitments.</p><h3>Can my contractor obtain the permits?</h3><p>The written agreement should identify who obtains necessary permits. Requirements vary by state and jurisdiction; for example, California CSLB guidance says home-improvement contracts need written direction about permit responsibility. Confirm the process with the governing agency and do not sign as owner-builder without understanding the resulting obligations.</p><h3>Who represents the homeowner?</h3><p>Each professional owes the duties established by their role and agreement; do not assume every participant provides independent homeowner advice. Read the agreements, ask directly about responsibilities and conflicts, and obtain legal advice about contractual rights when needed.</p><h3>Does Foundation recommend team members?</h3><p>No. Foundation does not select project professionals, maintain a preferred-provider list, accept referral compensation, or guarantee performance. Its role is to help homeowners organize information, understand choices, and prepare their own questions and decisions.</p></section>

<section class="article-conclusion"><h2>Build the Right Team, Not the Largest Team</h2><p>A well-structured renovation team makes both expertise and responsibility visible. Begin with the project's goals and complexity, verify qualifications and agreements, and resolve gaps before they become construction questions. Understanding each role helps you make better hiring decisions and creates a clearer path from planning through completion.</p><p>Not sure which roles your project needs?
