<p>An ADU places two households on one property, sometimes only a few feet apart or within the same building. Everyday sounds—conversation, televisions, footsteps, plumbing, doors and mechanical equipment—can affect privacy and comfort in both homes.</p>
<p>Sound control is most effective when it is considered during site planning and design. Finish materials alone rarely solve a poorly located or poorly coordinated condition, and no ordinary residential assembly can promise complete silence.</p>
<section id="identify-the-noise-sources-and-sensitive-rooms"><h2>Identify the Noise Sources and Sensitive Rooms</h2>
<p>Begin by identifying where sound is likely to originate and where it would be most disruptive. Kitchens, bathrooms, laundry areas, entries, stairs, televisions and outdoor gathering spaces may generate regular noise. Bedrooms, home offices and nurseries usually need more protection.</p>
<p>Consider both households. A quiet ADU bedroom may share a wall or face a patio used by the primary home, while the primary bedroom may be exposed to the ADU’s entrance, parking area or heat-pump equipment.</p>
</section>
<section id="use-site-placement-as-the-first-sound-control-tool"><h2>Use Site Placement as the First Sound-Control Tool</h2>
<p>Distance can help reduce sound, but orientation and intervening features are equally important. An ADU entrance located beside the primary bedroom may create more disturbance than a slightly closer entrance facing another direction.</p>
<p>Where the property allows, use garages, closets, bathrooms, storage rooms, fences, walls or landscaping as buffers between active and quiet areas. Outdoor equipment and gathering areas should be located with nearby windows and property uses in mind.</p>
</section>
<section id="separate-active-and-quiet-rooms"><h2>Separate Active and Quiet Rooms</h2>
<p>Room arrangement can reduce noise before specialized assemblies are considered. Avoid placing an ADU television wall, kitchen, laundry or bathroom directly against a bedroom in the primary home when a reasonable alternative exists.</p>
<p>Within the ADU, use closets, hallways, bathrooms or storage as buffers between living areas and bedrooms. In attached or converted ADUs, align similar uses where practical—for example, placing utility or bathroom areas next to existing service spaces rather than quiet rooms.</p>
</section>
<section id="understand-different-types-of-sound"><h2>Understand Different Types of Sound</h2>
<p>Sound reaches a room in several ways:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Airborne sound travels through the air, including voices, music and television noise.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Impact sound begins with physical contact, including footsteps, dropped objects and moving furniture.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Structure-borne vibration travels through framing and connected building components, including equipment vibration and plumbing movement.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Flanking sound bypasses the primary wall or floor through ceilings, floors, ducts, openings, framing connections or adjacent spaces.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A solution aimed at one pathway may not address the others. For example, adding insulation to a ceiling cavity may do little for footfall vibration if the floor assembly and structural connections remain unchanged.</p>
</section>
<section id="detached-and-attached-adus-have-different-challenges"><h2>Detached and Attached ADUs Have Different Challenges</h2>
<p>A detached ADU has physical separation from the primary home, but exterior openings, equipment, entries and outdoor activity can still transmit noise across the property. Window and door orientation may matter more than interior separation assemblies.</p>
<p>An attached ADU or conversion may share walls, floors, ceilings, framing and utility paths with the main home. These shared connections create more opportunities for sound and vibration to travel around or through the intended separation.</p>
</section>
<section id="plan-wall-assemblies-as-complete-systems"><h2>Plan Wall Assemblies as Complete Systems</h2>
<p>Effective sound-control walls generally use a combination of mass, cavity absorption, reduced structural coupling and careful sealing. The appropriate assembly depends on the project, available space, fire-resistance requirements, structure and performance goals.</p>
<p>More layers do not automatically guarantee better results. Framing configuration, fasteners, resilient components, insulation type, electrical boxes, perimeter joints and workmanship can all affect performance. The selected wall should be documented as a complete tested or professionally designed assembly where acoustic performance is important.</p>
</section>
<section id="floor-and-ceiling-assemblies-need-special-attention"><h2>Floor and Ceiling Assemblies Need Special Attention</h2>
<p>Stacked ADUs and garage conversions can be particularly sensitive to footfall and structure-borne noise. Floor finishes, underlayments, subfloor construction, framing, cavity insulation, ceiling attachment and penetrations work together.</p>
<p>Soft finishes may reduce some impact noise but may not fit the desired use or maintenance plan. Resilient ceiling components or isolated framing can help in some assemblies, but installation errors and rigid connections can reduce their effectiveness. Coordinate acoustic goals with structural, fire, ceiling-height and mechanical requirements.</p>
</section>
<section id="seal-gaps-and-penetrations"><h2>Seal Gaps and Penetrations</h2>
<p>Sound can travel through small openings that appear insignificant. Pay attention to wall and ceiling perimeters, electrical boxes, recessed fixtures, plumbing penetrations, duct openings, access panels and joints around doors or windows.</p>
<p>Sealing methods and materials must remain compatible with fire, smoke, moisture and movement requirements. Do not substitute generic caulk for a specified fire-rated or acoustical treatment.</p>
</section>
<section id="avoid-back-to-back-openings"><h2>Avoid Back-to-Back Openings</h2>
<p>Back-to-back electrical boxes, medicine cabinets, plumbing access panels or recessed features can weaken a separation assembly. Offset penetrations where practical and preserve the required layers around them.</p>
<p>Any recessed component should be coordinated before framing. A late-added niche or cabinet can remove insulation, interrupt sheathing or create a direct sound path.</p>
</section>
<section id="doors-and-windows-often-control-the-result"><h2>Doors and Windows Often Control the Result</h2>
<p>A high-performing wall provides limited benefit if it contains a lightweight, poorly sealed door. Door mass, perimeter seals, thresholds and installation quality affect privacy. Exterior windows and doors can also transmit sound between buildings even when the wall assemblies perform well.</p>
<p>Locate openings to avoid direct alignment between homes. Select products based on the specific noise concern and confirm that security, emergency escape, ventilation, accessibility and weather protection are not compromised.</p>
</section>
<section id="control-plumbing-noise"><h2>Control Plumbing Noise</h2>
<p>Drainage, water flow, pumps and pipe movement can be noticeable in small or quiet spaces. Avoid routing noisy plumbing through bedroom walls or above sleeping areas when alternatives are available.</p>
<p>Pipe sizing, supports, isolation, wall construction and access locations should be coordinated by the design and construction team. Oversized holes, rigid contact with framing and poorly secured piping can allow noise and vibration to travel through the building.</p>
</section>
<section id="locate-mechanical-equipment-thoughtfully"><h2>Locate Mechanical Equipment Thoughtfully</h2>
<p>Heat pumps, condensers, exhaust fans, water heaters and pumps can create both airborne sound and vibration. Published equipment ratings are useful, but actual experience also depends on mounting, clearances, operating mode, nearby walls and the location of windows or sleeping rooms.</p>
<p>Use appropriate pads, curbs, hangers or isolation components where specified. Do not mount vibrating equipment to a shared bedroom wall or lightweight structure without evaluating the resulting sound path.</p>
</section>
<section id="ducts-and-vents-can-become-sound-paths"><h2>Ducts and Vents Can Become Sound Paths</h2>
<p>Ductwork and shared cavities can carry sound between spaces. Avoid direct openings that connect the ADU and primary home unless the designed system specifically addresses separation, fire and acoustic requirements.</p>
<p>Exhaust outlets and outdoor equipment should also be located to reduce noise at nearby windows and patios. Duct routing, lining, transitions and equipment connections should be designed by qualified professionals where sound control is a significant concern.</p>
</section>
<section id="reduce-entry-parking-and-outdoor-noise"><h2>Reduce Entry, Parking and Outdoor Noise</h2>
<p>Sound privacy is influenced by more than the building envelope. Gates, footsteps, vehicle doors, trash containers, delivery activity and outdoor conversations can disturb nearby rooms.</p>
<p>Locate the ADU path and entry away from bedroom windows where feasible. Use durable, quiet gate hardware and avoid placing gathering areas or frequently used bins directly against sensitive walls.</p>
</section>
<section id="construction-quality-matters"><h2>Construction Quality Matters</h2>
<p>Acoustic assemblies are sensitive to workmanship. Missing insulation, compressed resilient components, unsealed perimeters, incorrectly attached drywall or unplanned penetrations can reduce expected performance.</p>
<p>Include important assembly details in the construction documents and inspect them before they are concealed. Trade coordination is essential because electrical, plumbing, mechanical and finish work can alter the assembly after it is framed.</p>
</section>
<section id="set-realistic-performance-expectations"><h2>Set Realistic Performance Expectations</h2>
<p>Sound ratings such as STC and IIC can help compare tested assemblies, but laboratory ratings do not guarantee the same result in a completed home. Field conditions, room geometry, frequency, background noise, flanking paths and occupant sensitivity all affect what residents hear.</p>
<p>Define the practical goal: reducing intelligible conversation, limiting television noise, controlling footsteps or minimizing equipment vibration. Different goals may require different solutions, and higher performance can affect wall thickness, ceiling height, cost and construction complexity.</p>
</section>
<section id="common-adu-sound-control-mistakes"><h2>Common ADU Sound-Control Mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Waiting until finishes are selected to address noise</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Locating entries, patios or equipment near bedroom windows</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Placing active rooms directly against quiet rooms without a buffer</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Assuming cavity insulation alone will solve sound transmission</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Ignoring floor impact noise in stacked or garage-conversion ADUs</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Allowing back-to-back boxes, access panels or recessed features</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Failing to seal assembly edges and penetrations</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Relying on a laboratory rating as a guarantee of field performance</p>
</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="foundation-tip"><h2>Foundation Tip</h2>
<p>Create a simple sound map of the property. Mark likely noise sources, quiet rooms and shared walls or floor-ceiling areas. Then review every path between them—including windows, doors, structure, plumbing, ducts and outdoor circulation—before selecting construction upgrades.</p>
</section>
<section id="how-foundation-can-help"><h2>How Foundation Can Help</h2>
<p>Foundation can help homeowners identify practical sound-control questions during ADU planning and construction review. The goal is to improve coordination among layout, assemblies, equipment, utilities and workmanship—not to promise a specific acoustic result or replace an acoustical consultant when specialized performance is required.</p>
</section>

Confirm current requirements and appropriate assemblies with qualified professionals and the applicable authorities.</p>
</section>
