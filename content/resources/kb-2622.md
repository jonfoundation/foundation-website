<p>Home addition proposals are rarely directly comparable. One contractor may include extensive work where the addition connects to the existing house, while another assumes only minimal repairs. One may include utility upgrades, site restoration and project supervision; another may exclude them or carry allowances.</p>
<p>The lowest proposal is not necessarily the lowest final project cost. Before selecting a contractor, homeowners need to understand what each proposal includes, where assumptions differ and which costs remain uncertain.</p>
<section id="why-home-addition-proposals-are-difficult-to-compare"><h2>Why Home Addition Proposals Are Difficult to Compare</h2>
<p>A home addition combines new construction with work inside and around an existing home. The project may involve demolition, temporary protection, foundations, structural connections, roof tie-ins, building systems, finishes, drainage, utility coordination and restoration of disturbed areas.</p>
<p>Contractors may interpret incomplete documents or undefined conditions differently. Even proposals prepared from the same drawings can reflect different assumptions about access, material quality, existing conditions, supervision and responsibility.</p>
<p>This means the stated totals may represent different versions of the project.</p>
</section>
<section id="begin-with-the-same-information"><h2>Begin With the Same Information</h2>
<p>Each contractor should price the same current drawings, specifications, consultant documents, finish information and written clarifications. If one contractor received additional information or priced a different revision, the proposals are not yet aligned.</p>
<p>Before comparison, confirm the basis of each proposal and ask contractors to update outdated or incomplete pricing.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-main-areas-that-deserve-attention"><h2>The Main Areas That Deserve Attention</h2>
<p>A meaningful review looks beyond the total and considers several categories:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Scope: What work is included, and where does the contractor’s responsibility begin and end?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Assumptions: What site, design, access or existing-condition assumptions support the price?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Allowances: Are placeholder amounts realistic for the homeowner’s intended quality and selections?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Exclusions: Which necessary project costs will be paid elsewhere or added later?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Schedule: What conditions must occur before the work starts, and how realistic is the proposed duration?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Project management: What supervision, communication, protection and closeout services are included?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Contract terms: How will payments, changes, delays, warranties and disputes be handled?</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A proposal can be detailed in one category and vague in another. The purpose of review is to identify those differences before they become contract assumptions.</p>
</section>
<section id="addition-specific-scope-gaps"><h2>Addition-Specific Scope Gaps</h2>
<p>The most important differences are often not the visible finishes. They are the work required to connect the addition to the existing house and property.</p>
<p>Areas that frequently need clarification include:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Demolition and repairs inside the existing home.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Temporary structural support and weather protection.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Foundation assumptions and site access.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Roofing, waterproofing and exterior-material transitions.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Electrical, plumbing, HVAC and utility capacity or service upgrades.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Protection of occupied areas and household access.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Grading, drainage, landscaping and site restoration.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Permit corrections, inspections, testing and closeout.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>If these items are not clearly addressed, a low proposal may simply defer costs that will still be necessary.</p>
</section>
<section id="allowances-can-distort-the-comparison"><h2>Allowances Can Distort the Comparison</h2>
<p>An allowance is a placeholder for an item or scope that is not fully defined. Allowances are useful, but they can make one proposal appear lower when it assumes a different quality level or excludes related labor, delivery, taxes, waste or markup.</p>
<p>Homeowners should understand what each allowance covers and whether it reflects the selections they are likely to make. The goal is not to eliminate every allowance; it is to avoid treating unequal allowances as equal pricing.</p>
</section>
<section id="exclusions-are-not-automatically-a-problem"><h2>Exclusions Are Not Automatically a Problem</h2>
<p>A clear exclusion can be more useful than an unclear inclusion. Some contractors reasonably exclude design fees, utility-company charges, hazardous-material remediation, owner-purchased products or work that cannot yet be defined.</p>
<p>The homeowner still needs to determine whether the excluded item is required, who will provide it and where it appears in the overall project budget. An exclusion becomes dangerous when it is mistaken for a savings.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-schedule-and-management-plan-matter"><h2>The Schedule and Management Plan Matter</h2>
<p>A home addition affects an occupied property for an extended period. Proposal review should consider who will manage the site, how often supervision will be present, how the contractor will communicate, what temporary protections are included and how the household will function during construction.</p>
<p>A shorter promised duration is not automatically better. It should be supported by a credible sequence, permit assumptions, trade availability and procurement plan.</p>
</section>
<section id="do-not-normalize-proposals-informally"><h2>Do Not Normalize Proposals Informally</h2>
<p>Homeowners often try to compare bids by adding an estimated amount for one contractor’s exclusion, subtracting another contractor’s allowance and creating a revised total. This can be directionally useful, but it can also create false precision.</p>
<p>Proposal language is interconnected. Adding a missing line item does not always account for related labor, supervision, markup, schedule effects or contract responsibility. Clarifications should be obtained from the contractors and incorporated into the final proposal or agreement.</p>
</section>
<section id="questions-are-more-valuable-than-assumptions"><h2>Questions Are More Valuable Than Assumptions</h2>
<p>When a proposal is unclear, ask a focused written question. The contractor’s response should identify whether the work is included, excluded, carried as an allowance or dependent on additional information.</p>
<p>The quality of the response also provides useful information about the contractor’s understanding of the project, communication style and willingness to define responsibility.</p>
</section>
<section id="warning-signs"><h2>Warning Signs</h2>
<ul>
<li>
<p>A price materially below the others without a clear explanation.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Major work described only with vague terms such as “standard” or “as needed.”</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Allowances that appear inconsistent with the expected design quality.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Little attention to existing-home repairs, utilities or site restoration.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>A schedule that does not address permits, long-lead items or occupied-home logistics.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Pressure to sign before clarifications are documented.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>No clear process for changes, payments, supervision or closeout.</p>
</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="when-a-professional-bid-review-is-worthwhile"><h2>When a Professional Bid Review Is Worthwhile</h2>
<p>Independent review can be especially valuable when proposals use different formats, the totals vary significantly, the addition affects multiple building systems, allowances are substantial or the homeowner is unsure which questions to ask.</p>
<p>A professional review should not choose the contractor for the homeowner. It should organize the information, identify material differences and help the homeowner make a more informed decision.</p>
</section>
